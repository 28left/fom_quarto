Toposes and Intuitionistic Logic

Truth values, Heyting algebras, and the internal language of a topos

Author
Affiliation

Jan Reimann

Penn State — Math 558

In \(\mathcal{S}\), parts of \(X\) correspond to maps \(X \to 2\), and the propositional connectives are maps \(2 \times 2 \to 2\) — the truth tables (L–R, Prop. 3.18). In a topos, \(2\) is replaced by \(\Omega\), and the parts of an object no longer form a Boolean algebra but a Heyting algebra. These notes show that this is not an accident of a few examples: the logic of every topos is intuitionistic, and intuitionistic logic is exactly the logic that is valid in all toposes.

From truth tables to \(\Omega\)

Recall from L–R, Chapter 3: a part \(A \subseteq X\) has a characteristic map \(\chi_A : X \to 2\), and a truth table such as \(\& : 2 \times 2 \to 2\) acts on parts by composition: \[ X \xrightarrow{\ \langle \chi_A, \chi_B \rangle\ } 2 \times 2 \xrightarrow{\ \&\ } 2 \qquad \text{classifies} \qquad A \cap B . \] In a topos \(\mathcal{E}\), the same picture holds with \(\Omega\) in place of \(2\): \[ \operatorname{Sub}(X) \;\cong\; \mathcal{E}(X, \Omega), \] and operations on parts that are natural in \(X\) are induced by maps \(\Omega \times \Omega \to \Omega\). We have already seen, in the Sierpiński topos and in \(\mathrm{Sh}(\mathbb{R})\), that these operations do not satisfy excluded middle. The question is: what laws do they satisfy?

Intuitionistic logic and Heyting algebras

Intuitionistic logic

Intuitionistic propositional logic has the usual connectives \(\wedge, \vee, \to, \bot\), with \(\neg \varphi\) defined as \(\varphi \to \bot\), and the usual natural deduction rules — except that the law of excluded middle \(\varphi \vee \neg\varphi\) (equivalently, double negation elimination \(\neg\neg\varphi \to \varphi\)) is not a rule.

The informal reading behind it is the BHK interpretation (Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov), which we develop in Week 10:

  • a proof of \(\varphi \wedge \psi\) is a proof of \(\varphi\) together with a proof of \(\psi\);
  • a proof of \(\varphi \vee \psi\) is a proof of one of them, together with the information which one;
  • a proof of \(\varphi \to \psi\) is a method transforming proofs of \(\varphi\) into proofs of \(\psi\);
  • a proof of \(\exists x\, \varphi(x)\) provides a witness \(a\) and a proof of \(\varphi(a)\).

On this reading, \(\varphi \vee \neg\varphi\) would require a uniform method deciding every proposition, so there is no reason to expect it.

valid intuitionistically valid only classically
\(\varphi \to \neg\neg\varphi\) \(\neg\neg\varphi \to \varphi\)
\(\neg\neg\neg\varphi \leftrightarrow \neg\varphi\) \(\varphi \vee \neg\varphi\)
\((\varphi \to \psi) \to (\neg\psi \to \neg\varphi)\) \((\neg\psi \to \neg\varphi) \to (\varphi \to \psi)\)
\(\neg(\varphi \vee \psi) \leftrightarrow \neg\varphi \wedge \neg\psi\) \(\neg(\varphi \wedge \psi) \to \neg\varphi \vee \neg\psi\)
\(\neg\neg(\varphi \vee \neg\varphi)\) \(((\varphi \to \psi) \to \varphi) \to \varphi\) (Peirce)

The last entry on the left is worth pausing on: excluded middle is not provable, but it is not refutable either.

Heyting algebras

NoteDefinition: Heyting algebra

A Heyting algebra is a lattice \(H\) with least element \(0\) and greatest element \(1\), together with an operation \(\Rightarrow\) such that for all \(a, b, c \in H\) \[ c \le (a \Rightarrow b) \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad c \wedge a \le b . \] We put \(\neg a := a \Rightarrow 0\), the largest element disjoint from \(a\).

The defining condition should look familiar. It is the exponential law \[ \frac{X \longrightarrow Y^B}{X \times B \longrightarrow Y} \] for a poset regarded as a category, where \(\times\) is \(\wedge\) and \(Y^B\) is \(B \Rightarrow Y\). So:

A Heyting algebra is exactly a cartesian closed poset with finite joins.

Many facts then come for free from the exponentials lecture. For instance, \(a \wedge -\) is a left adjoint, so it preserves joins: every Heyting algebra is distributive, just as exponentials forced distributivity of \(\times\) over \(+\).

A Boolean algebra is a Heyting algebra in which \(a \vee \neg a = 1\) for all \(a\) (equivalently, \(\neg\neg a = a\)).

Examples.

  1. Every Boolean algebra, with \(a \Rightarrow b = \neg a \vee b\); in particular \(2\).
  2. The open sets \(\mathcal{O}(X)\) of a topological space, with \(U \Rightarrow V = \operatorname{int}\big((X \setminus U) \cup V\big)\) and \(\neg U = \operatorname{int}(X \setminus U)\).
  3. Any finite chain \(0 < a_1 < \dots < 1\), with \(a \Rightarrow b = 1\) if \(a \le b\) and \(a \Rightarrow b = b\) otherwise. The three-element chain \(\{\bot, \tfrac12, \top\}\) is the stage-\(0\) truth values of the Sierpiński topos: \(\neg \tfrac12 = \bot\), \(\neg\neg\tfrac12 = \top\), and \(\tfrac12 \vee \neg\tfrac12 = \tfrac12\).

Theorem 20.1 (Soundness and completeness)
A propositional formula is provable in intuitionistic logic if and only if it evaluates to \(1\) under every assignment in every Heyting algebra. In fact, the single Heyting algebra \(\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{R})\) suffices (Tarski).

Compare: classical propositional logic is complete for Boolean algebras, and already for the two-element algebra \(2\) — the truth tables.

The logic of parts in a topos

Throughout, \(\operatorname{Sub}(X)\) is ordered by inclusion of parts: for parts \(i : A \rightarrowtail X\) and \(j : B \rightarrowtail X\), \[ i \subseteq_X j \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad i = j k \text{ for some (necessarily unique) } k : A \to B . \] On monos this is only a preorder; two monos that factor through each other are isomorphic over \(X\), and \(\operatorname{Sub}(X)\) consists of these equivalence classes, on which \(\subseteq_X\) is a partial order. In terms of characteristic maps, \(A \subseteq_X B\) iff \(\chi_A \wedge \chi_B = \chi_A\).

WarningInclusion is tested at all stages

In \(\mathcal{S}\), \(A \subseteq_X B\) can be checked with elements \(1 \to X\). In a general topos one needs generalized elements \(T \to X\). In the Sierpiński topos, the parts \((\varnothing, 1)\) and \(0\) of \(1\) contain the same global elements (none), yet they are different.

Theorem 20.2 (Parts form Heyting algebras)
Let \(\mathcal{E}\) be a topos.

  1. For every object \(X\), \(\operatorname{Sub}(X)\) is a Heyting algebra.
  2. For every \(f : Y \to X\), pullback \(f^* : \operatorname{Sub}(X) \to \operatorname{Sub}(Y)\) preserves all the Heyting operations, and it has both adjoints: \[ \exists_f \;\dashv\; f^* \;\dashv\; \forall_f . \]

Concretely, the top and bottom of \(\operatorname{Sub}(X)\) are \(1_X\) and \(0 \rightarrowtail X\); the meet \(A \cap B\) is the pullback; the join \(A \cup B\) is the image of \(A + B \to X\); and the implication \(A \Rightarrow B\), the largest part \(C\) with \(C \cap A \subseteq_X B\), is \(\forall_i(i^* B)\) for \(i : A \rightarrowtail X\).

In \(\mathcal{S}\), for \(B \subseteq Y\) and \(f : Y \to X\): \[ \exists_f(B) = \{ x : \exists y\,( f(y) = x \wedge y \in B)\}, \qquad \forall_f(B) = \{ x : \forall y\,( f(y) = x \to y \in B)\}. \] For a projection \(\pi : X \times Y \to X\) these are literally the quantifiers \(\exists y\) and \(\forall y\). This is Lawvere’s slogan “quantifiers are adjoints”: the rules \[ \frac{\exists y\, \varphi(x,y) \;\vdash\; \psi(x)}{\varphi(x,y) \;\vdash\; \psi(x)} \qquad\qquad \frac{\psi(x) \;\vdash\; \forall y\, \varphi(x,y)}{\psi(x) \;\vdash\; \varphi(x,y)} \] (with \(y\) not free in \(\psi\)) are exactly the two adjunctions.

The operations are maps on \(\Omega\). Because \(\operatorname{Sub}(X) \cong \mathcal{E}(X,\Omega)\) naturally in \(X\), the Heyting operations are induced by maps \[ \wedge,\ \vee,\ \Rightarrow\ : \ \Omega \times \Omega \to \Omega, \qquad \bot : 1 \to \Omega, \] making \(\Omega\) an internal Heyting algebra — the topos version of the truth tables. For instance, \(\wedge\) classifies the part \(\langle \mathrm{true}, \mathrm{true}\rangle : 1 \rightarrowtail \Omega \times \Omega\), and \(\Rightarrow\) classifies the order relation \(\{(p, q) : p \wedge q = p\} \subseteq \Omega \times \Omega\).

In the examples.

  • In \(\mathrm{Sh}(X)\): \(\Omega(U) = \mathcal{O}(U)\) with the Heyting operations of open sets, computed on each \(U\).
  • In presheaves on \(C\): on sieves, \(\wedge\) and \(\vee\) are intersection and union, and \[ (S \Rightarrow T) = \{ f : d \to c \;:\; \text{for all } g,\ \ f g \in S \Rightarrow f g \in T \}. \]
  • In \(\mathcal{S}/X\), \(\mathcal{S}^G\): Boolean, computed fiberwise or on underlying sets.

The internal language

The correspondence between parts and formulas can be pushed all the way: every topos comes with an internal language (the Mitchell–Bénabou language).

  • Types are objects of \(\mathcal{E}\); terms \(t(x)\) with \(x : X\) of type \(Y\) are maps \(X \to Y\).
  • A formula \(\varphi(x)\) with \(x : X\) is interpreted as a part \(\{ x : \varphi \} \subseteq X\), or equivalently a map \(X \to \Omega\).
  • Connectives are interpreted by the Heyting operations of \(\operatorname{Sub}(X)\); quantifiers by \(\exists_\pi\) and \(\forall_\pi\) along projections; and \(\Omega\), \(Y^X\) give higher-order quantification (“for all subsets”, “for all functions”).
  • A sentence holds in \(\mathcal{E}\) if its interpretation is the top part, i.e. \(\mathrm{true}: 1 \to \Omega\).

Theorem 20.3 (Toposes model intuitionistic higher-order logic)
Every statement provable in intuitionistic higher-order logic holds in every topos. Conversely, a statement holding in all toposes is intuitionistically provable: every theory has a “free” topos built from its syntax (Lambek–Scott).

Slogan. Toposes are to intuitionistic higher-order logic what Boolean-valued models are to classical logic. A mathematical argument that avoids excluded middle and choice is valid in every topos — for sheaves, \(G\)-sets, the effective topos, … all at once.

Local truth: Kripke–Joyal semantics

How do we actually compute whether a formula holds? For \(\mathrm{Sh}(X)\), write \(U \Vdash \varphi\) (“\(U\) forces \(\varphi\)”) for \(U \subseteq [\![\varphi]\!]\). Then:

\(U \Vdash \ldots\) iff
\(\varphi \wedge \psi\) \(U \Vdash \varphi\) and \(U \Vdash \psi\)
\(\varphi \vee \psi\) there is an open cover \(U = \bigcup_i U_i\) with each \(U_i \Vdash \varphi\) or \(U_i \Vdash \psi\)
\(\varphi \to \psi\) for all open \(V \subseteq U\): if \(V \Vdash \varphi\) then \(V \Vdash \psi\)
\(\neg \varphi\) the only open \(V \subseteq U\) with \(V \Vdash \varphi\) is \(V = \varnothing\)
\(\exists x\, \varphi(x)\) there is a cover \(U = \bigcup_i U_i\) and sections \(a_i \in F(U_i)\) with \(U_i \Vdash \varphi(a_i)\)
\(\forall x\, \varphi(x)\) for all open \(V \subseteq U\) and all \(a \in F(V)\): \(V \Vdash \varphi(a)\)

Two features stand out:

  1. Disjunction and existence are local. A disjunction holds if it holds piece by piece; an existential statement holds if witnesses exist locally, which need not glue to a global one.
  2. Implication and negation look ahead to all smaller open sets, exactly as in Kripke models (and in the Sierpiński topos, where “\(\neg\)” looks ahead to the later stage).

Example: square roots. In \(\mathrm{Sh}(\mathbb{C}\setminus \{0\})\), let \(z\) be the identity function, a global section of the sheaf of continuous complex-valued functions. Every point has a slit-plane neighborhood on which a continuous square root of \(z\) exists, so \[ \mathbb{C}\setminus \{0\} \;\Vdash\; \exists w\, (w \cdot w = z), \] although there is no global square root (see the presheaves and sheaves handout). Internally, “\(z\) has a square root” is true; externally there is no element to point to. This is the intuitionistic meaning of \(\exists\), and it is why internal versions of the axiom of choice can fail even when every \(\forall\exists\) statement we care about holds.

TipConnection to Week 5

These clauses are the forcing clauses of Cohen’s method, with open sets in place of forcing conditions: a condition forces \(\varphi \vee \psi\) if densely many stronger conditions force one of them, and forces \(\neg\varphi\) if no stronger condition forces \(\varphi\). Forcing is sheaf semantics.

Where classical logic lives

Theorem 20.4 (Classical toposes)
For a topos \(\mathcal{E}\) the following are equivalent:

  1. \(\mathcal{E}\) is Boolean: every \(\operatorname{Sub}(X)\) is a Boolean algebra;
  2. \([\mathrm{true}, \mathrm{false}] : 1 + 1 \to \Omega\) is an isomorphism;
  3. the internal language satisfies excluded middle, \(\forall p \in \Omega\,(p \vee \neg p)\).

Moreover (Diaconescu), the internal axiom of choice implies these.

Even in a non-Boolean topos, classical logic is not far away. Every topos \(\mathcal{E}\) contains a Boolean subtopos \(\mathrm{Sh}_{\neg\neg}(\mathcal{E})\) of “\(\neg\neg\)-sheaves”, where a statement holds iff it is not refuted. Applied to presheaves on a poset of forcing conditions, this is exactly how the Cohen topos is built — the categorical version of the passage from forcing to a Boolean-valued model. It mirrors the Gödel–Gentzen double negation translation, which embeds classical logic into intuitionistic logic.

In a topos, intuitionistic logic is not a philosophical restriction imposed from outside: it is forced by the fact that truth is local — it varies over stages, points, or conditions.

Outlook: Week 10

A Heyting algebra is a cartesian closed poset. Forgetting that the order is a preorder — keeping track of which proof shows \(a \le b\), not just that one exists — gives a cartesian closed category. This is the categorical side of the Curry–Howard correspondence: propositions are types, proofs are programs, and the BHK clauses above become the typing rules of the simply typed \(\lambda\)-calculus.

Exercises

Exercise 20.1 In any Heyting algebra, prove: (a) \(a \le \neg\neg a\); (b) \(\neg\neg\neg a = \neg a\); (c) \(\neg(a \vee b) = \neg a \wedge \neg b\); (d) \(\neg\neg(a \vee \neg a) = 1\).

Exercise 20.2 In \(\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{R})\), find open sets \(U, V\) with \(\neg(U \cap V) \neq \neg U \cup \neg V\), and an open \(U\) with \(\neg\neg U \neq U\). Use your examples to show that the De Morgan law and double negation elimination from the right-hand column of the table of laws are not intuitionistically provable.

Exercise 20.3 Write out the truth tables of \(\wedge\), \(\vee\), \(\Rightarrow\), \(\neg\) on the three-element chain \(\{\bot, \tfrac12, \top\}\). Check that Peirce’s law fails for \(\varphi = \tfrac12\), \(\psi = \bot\).

Exercise 20.4 In \(\mathcal{S}\), verify \(\exists_f \dashv f^* \dashv \forall_f\) for \(f : Y \to X\), and check that for a projection \(\pi : X \times Y \to X\) they give the usual quantifiers.

Exercise 20.5 Using the Kripke–Joyal clauses, show that in \(\mathrm{Sh}(\mathbb{R})\), with \(x\) the identity function, \(\mathbb{R}\Vdash \neg\neg(x > 0 \vee x < 0)\) but not \(\mathbb{R}\Vdash x > 0 \vee x < 0\).
