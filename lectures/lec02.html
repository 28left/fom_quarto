The Foundational Crisis

From Paradox to Program

Recap and Roadmap

Last time: Dedekind & Cantor’s existential, set-theoretic style of mathematics — and Russell’s paradox, which broke Frege’s comprehension principle.

Today

  • paradoxes and consistency, predicativity, the axiom of choice
  • Brouwer’s intuitionism vs. Hilbert’s program

The question throughout: what is a legitimate mathematical object, definition, or proof?

The Comprehension Principle, Again

Recall the principle that broke under Russell’s paradox:

\[ \text{for any well-defined property } p,\ \text{there exists } \{x : p(x)\} \]

  • This was the basis for the logicist understanding of set theory — “naive” set theory
  • Not just Russell’s paradox: two families of paradoxes emerged around 1900
    • set-theoretic paradoxes (Burali-Forti, Cantor, Russell/Zermelo)
    • semantic paradoxes (Richard, König, Grelling) — about truth and definability

The Set-Theoretic Paradoxes

  • Burali-Forti (1897; Cantor 1896): the set of all ordinals would itself be well-ordered, hence have an ordinal \(>\) every ordinal in it — contradiction
  • Cantor (1899): the set of all cardinals leads to the same kind of contradiction
  • Zermelo–Russell (1901–02): let \(p(x)\) be “\(x \notin x\)” and consider \(R = \{x : x \notin x\}\)

Comprehension is contradictory in the simplest possible way — no need for a theory of the transfinite to state it.

Hilbert and Existence

Hilbert abandoned logicism. He proposed working with axiom systems that comprise non-logical axioms.

This, however, requires a new kind of justification why the ojects we are trying to axiomatize exist.

Hilbert’s proposal:

  • A set of objects “exists” only if we can prove its defining axiom system consistent
  • concept \(\to\) set becomes consistency proof \(\to\) legitimate existence
  • Objects have a kind of ideal existence “in the realm of thought,” not independent metaphysical existence

Predicativity — Poincaré’s Diagnosis

When Frege’s and Russell’s paradoxes became widely known (1903), Poincaré offered his own culprit: impredicative definitions.

A definition is impredicative when it refers to a totality that already contains the very element being defined.

  • Example: least upper bound – defining the supremum involves quantifying over “all upper bounds,” a set of real numbers, in order to pin down a particular real number. That real number is thus specified by reference to a totality it belongs to.
  • Poincaré: this kind of circularity is exactly what licenses the paradoxes

Richard’s Paradox (a Semantic Paradox)

  • Only countably many reals are definable by a finite expression — list them \(a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots\) (lexicographic order)
  • Define \(r\): the \(n\)th decimal digit of \(r\) differs from the \(n\)th digit of \(a_n\)
  • \(r\) is not on the list — yet \(r\) was just defined, in finitely many words!

Poincaré’s diagnosis: \(r\)’s definition refers to the totality of all definable numbers, of which \(r\) is itself meant to be a member. Impredicative.

Two Responses to Impredicativity

Zermelo — unconvinced

  • Impredicative definitions are everywhere in classical analysis (e.g. least upper bound)
  • Harmless: the object isn’t created by the definition, only singled out from what already exists

Russell — convinced

  • Adopts Poincaré’s “vicious circle principle”
  • Builds a theory of types: objects stratified into levels; no set of “mixed” type
  • Result: Principia Mathematica (1910–13)

Where Predicativity Led

  • Russell’s ramified type theory needed extra ad hoc machinery (the axiom of reducibility) to recover ordinary mathematics
  • Weyl, in Das Kontinuum (1918), tried a leaner predicative foundation: accept classical logic and \(\mathbb{N}\), but build the reals predicatively
    • cost: \(\mathbb{R}\) is no longer complete — the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem fails
  • Predicative mathematics survives today as a serious middle ground between classical and constructive mathematics

Controversy on the Axiom of Choice

Zermelo (1904): \(\mathrm{AC} \implies\) every set — in particular \(\mathbb{R}\) — can be well-ordered.

  • \(\mathrm{AC}\): given any family of disjoint nonempty sets, a choice set exists containing one element from each
  • The proof gives no explicit construction of the well-ordering
  • Critics: \(\mathrm{AC}\) merely asserts the choice set exists, without a defining property for it

\(\Rightarrow\) a deep debate followed, cutting across the logicism/predicativism divide.

From Controversy to Axiom System

The AC debate spurred Zermelo to work out his axiom system for set theory (1908) — a careful, mathematical (not logical) analysis of Cantor’s and Dedekind’s set theory.

  • Fraenkel & von Neumann (1920s) add: Replacement and Regularity
  • Weyl & Skolem: formalize within first-order logic — quantify only over sets, not over properties

\[ \rightsquigarrow \quad \mathrm{ZF(C)} \]

What ZFC Codifies

ZFC settles the early-1900s controversies by simply building in the modern, anti-Kroneckerian traits:

  • impredicative definitions — allowed
  • arbitrary functions and subsets — allowed
  • purely existential proofs — allowed

The result: a working “Platonistic” view of mathematics — the objects a theory refers to are treated as if they exist independently of what a mathematician can explicitly define or construct.

By the 1920s, Russell’s (simplified) type theory and Zermelo’s set theory had largely converged on this same methodology.

Summary - Response to the Paradoxes

The paradoxes did not kill modern mathematics.

  • They forced a clarification: existence via consistency, not naive comprehension
  • Poincaré’s predicativism and Russell’s type theory tried to restrain the new methods
  • Zermelo’s set theory — soon ZFC — codified the new methods instead.

A New Kind of Critique

1921: Weyl publishes a paper in the Mathematische Zeitschrift — a disciple of Hilbert’s — openly siding with Brouwer, diagnosing a “crisis in the foundations.”

  • Weyl’s paper: deliberately a propaganda pamphlet, “to rouse the sleepers”
  • Hilbert’s reply, same year: Brouwer and Weyl are attempting a “putsch” — a “dictatorship à la Kronecker”

The foundational debate turns into open battle: Hilbert’s classical mathematics vs. Brouwer’s constructive “revolution.”

What is Brouwer’s “Revolution”?

Up to 1920, the debate concerned: real numbers, impredicativity, and the strong existential assumptions of set theory (esp. AC).

Brouwer went further:

  • He put classical logic itself on trial: quantifiers over infinite domains, and the principle of excluded middle (PEM)
  • Poincaré, Klein, and others had said logic is not the whole of mathematics; Brouwer said mathematics is prior to and independent of logic and language altogether.

Intuitionism — First Principles

  • For Brouwer, mathematical objects are mental constructions; mathematics does not depend on language or logic to exist
  • Classical logic (including PEM) was abstracted from experience with finite sets — and wrongly extrapolated to infinite ones
  • PEM: \(p \vee \neg p\) — “either \(p\) or not \(p\)” — always holds, classically

Brouwer’s claim: this is not a law of thought, but an unjustified generalization.

The Constructive Standard

Consequence: proof by contradiction is invalid.

  • Classically: assume \(\neg p\), derive a contradiction, conclude \(p\)
  • Constructively: this only shows \(\neg\neg p\) — not the same as a construction of \(p\)

A Different Notion of Truth

For a constructivist:

  • “\(p\) is true” means: we possess a proof of \(p\) by stringent constructive methods
  • “\(p\) is false” means: we possess a counterexample to \(p\)

To a constructivist, the statement “π is normal” currently has no truth value — not true, not false, not “unknown-but-determinate.” Truth is not timeless; it is made.

Rebuilding Analysis: Choice Sequences

Brouwer (1918) replaces Cantorian sets with constructive substitutes: spreads and species.

  • A choice sequence: a sequence of rationals not required to be fully specified in advance
    • may be generated by a fixed rule (e.g. Newton’s method for \(\sqrt 2\))
    • or by choices the mathematician makes freely, over time, subject to constraints

Intuitionistic mathematics is “mathematics in the making” — unlike the timeless, fully-determinate objects of classical analysis.

Spreads and a New Continuum

  • A spread is a law regulating which choice sequences are admissible — the basis for Brouwer’s reconstruction of \(\mathbb{R}\)
  • The continuum becomes a genuinely continuous domain, not a set of pre-existing points
    • an echo of Aristotle: the continuum is not made up of unextended points

A function on a spread must be determined from a finite initial segment of any choice sequence — this has a startling consequence.

The Startling Consequence

Every intuitionistically total function on \(\mathbb{R}\) is (uniformly) continuous.

  • The classical step function \[ f(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & x \leq 0 \\ 1 & x > 0 \end{cases} \] is not a legitimate function for Brouwer
  • Reason: deciding \(x > 0\) vs. \(x \leq 0\) is not always possible — not decidable from a finite stage of the sequence

(Anticipates the later, independent discovery that all computable real functions are continuous — Turing and others.)

The Cost of Intuitionism

Rebuilding analysis this way is hard. Weyl, who once championed Brouwer’s program, wrote in 1925:

“The mathematician watches with pain the largest part of his towering theories dissolve into mist before his eyes.”

Brouwer himself, unfazed, replied (1933):

“The spheres of truth are less transparent than those of illusion.”

Weyl abandoned intuitionism shortly after. But Hilbert had another answer in mind.

Hilbert’s Program

Hilbert (1928): to “eliminate from the world once and for all the skeptical doubts” about classical mathematics.

  • Strategy: use Kroneckerian (finitary, constructive) means to justify anti-Kroneckerian (classical) methods
  • Key move: turn proofs into formal, syntactic objects — finite strings of symbols manipulated by explicit rules
  • A proof becomes something a finitary, combinatorial argument can reason about, from the outside — metamathematics

The Four Steps of the Program

  1. Find suitable axioms and primitive concepts for a theory \(T\) (e.g. the real numbers)
  2. Find axioms and rules for classical logic — syntactic rules turning propositions into new propositions
  3. Formalize \(T\): propositions become strings of symbols, proofs become sequences of strings obeying formal rules
  4. Give a finitary proof that no string expressing a contradiction can ever be the last line of a proof

If (iv) succeeds for \(T\): consistency is established by means even a strict finitist should accept.

Finitism: What Counts as “Given”

Hilbert’s answer to Poincaré’s 1905 circularity worry (a consistency proof for arithmetic would need induction):

  • What is genuinely, intuitively given: signs and formulas themselves, as concrete finite objects
  • Metamathematical reasoning about them needs only a very weak form of induction — much weaker than full arithmetical induction
  • Finitary mathematics needs no further justification — it is where justification bottoms out

Progress and Limits (1920s)

  • Propositional logic: proved consistent and complete
  • First-order (predicate) logic: proved complete — Gödel’s dissertation, 1929
  • Ackermann, von Neumann: consistency results for subsystems of arithmetic (1928–1930)
  • By 1930 Hilbert believed full consistency of arithmetic was within reach

A crucial hidden assumption: that the axiomatic systems in question would also be complete.

“Formalism” — a Slightly Misleading Name

  • The label comes from Hilbert’s method: formalize a theory, study its formal proof-structure
  • But Hilbert did not think mathematics is a meaningless game with symbols
    • he consistently stressed the meaningfulness of informal mathematical statements
  • Unlike intuitionism, formalism was never a full philosophy of mathematics — it was a method for justifying the classical one

A Personal Crisis, Too

The dispute was not only intellectual.

  • Hilbert and Brouwer: strong-willed, brilliant, and deeply incompatible personalities
  • 1928: Hilbert moves to remove Brouwer from the editorial board of Mathematische Annalen
    • Einstein: the move is “unwise” and wants no part of it
    • The board is dissolved and reconstituted without Brouwer — the journal loses Einstein and Carathéodory
  • Brouwer, disturbed, withdraws from the scene for years — as political tensions fade, so does the sense of “crisis”

Looking Ahead



Hilbert’s program looked to be within reach by 1930 — one more push on the consistency of arithmetic.
