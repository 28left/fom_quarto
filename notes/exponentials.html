Mapping Sets and Exponentials

The last topos axiom — exponentiation as a natural bijection, functoriality, the exponential laws, and the definition of an elementary topos

Author
Affiliation

Jan Reimann

Penn State — Math 558

Reading: Lawvere–Rosebrugh, Sets for Mathematics, Chapter 5 (pp. 96–110). So far we have required of the category \(\mathcal{S}\) of abstract sets and mappings: a terminal object, finite limits (products, equalizers, pullbacks), and a truth-value object \(2\) that classifies parts. One axiom is missing before we can say what a topos is: the existence of mapping sets \(Y^B\). Today we add it, and we will see that the tools from last time — functors and natural transformations — are exactly the language in which this axiom is most naturally stated.

Where we are

The axioms on \(\mathcal{S}\) so far (L–R, Ch. 1–3):

\(\mathcal{S}\) is a category
Associative composition, identities.
Terminal set \(1\); initial set \(0\); \(1\) separates mappings
Elements of \(A\) are maps \(1 \to A\), and maps are determined by their values on elements.
Binary sums
\(A + B\) with injections, universal among pairs \(A \to Y\), \(B \to Y\).
Finite inverse limits
Products and equalizers (hence pullbacks and all finite limits).
Membership via truth values
Every part of \(A\) has a unique characteristic map \(A \to 2\), and every \(A \to 2\) is one.

Every one of these axioms has the same shape: an object is introduced together with a bijection between two kinds of maps. For products, maps into \(Y_0 \times Y_1\) correspond to pairs of maps; for sums, maps out of \(A + B\) correspond to pairs of maps; for \(2\), maps into \(2\) correspond to parts. Chapter 5 makes this shape explicit and then uses it for one more construction.

The two-level pattern, illustrated by products

L–R write the defining property of the product as a “fraction” \[ \frac{\;X \longrightarrow Y_0 \times Y_1\;}{\;X \longrightarrow Y_0, \quad X \longrightarrow Y_1\;} \] meaning: there is a natural process turning a map of the upper kind into one of the lower kind, a natural process in the other direction, and the two are mutually inverse.

Level 1 (crude): “there is such an invertible process, for all \(X, Y_0, Y_1\).”

Level 2 (refined): the process is carried out by a functorial construction plus composition with one specific map. And the specific map is obtained by bootstrapping: feed the crude process an identity map.

  • Downward. Take \(X = Y_0 \times Y_1\) and put \(1_{Y_0\times Y_1}\) above the bar. Below the bar we get two specific maps — the projections \(p_0, p_1\). The general downward process is then composition with them: \(f \mapsto (p_0 f, \, p_1 f)\).
  • Upward. Take \(Y_0 = Y_1 = X\) and put \((1_X, 1_X)\) below the bar. Above the bar we get a specific map, the diagonal \(\delta_X = \langle 1_X, 1_X\rangle : X \to X \times X\). Using the functoriality of \(\times\) on maps, \[ (f_0 \times f_1)\langle x_0, x_1\rangle = \langle f_0 x_0, f_1 x_1 \rangle, \qquad (g_0 \times g_1)(f_0 \times f_1) = (g_0 f_0) \times (g_1 f_1), \] the general upward process is \[ \langle f_0, f_1 \rangle = (f_0 \times f_1)\,\delta_X . \] (Check it by composing both sides with \(p_0\) and \(p_1\).)

The pattern. Universal property \(=\) natural bijection of hom-sets. Applying the bijection to an identity produces a universal map (here \(p_0, p_1\) and \(\delta_X\)), and the general bijection is “apply the functor, then compose with the universal map.”

We now run exactly this program for a new construction.

Exponentiation

The crude idea

For sets \(B\), \(Y\) we want a set \(Y^B\) with \[ \frac{\;X \longrightarrow Y^B\;}{\;X \times B \longrightarrow Y\;} \] natural in \(X\) (and \(Y\)). Bootstrap immediately with \(X = 1\): since \(1 \times B \cong B\), \[ \frac{\;1 \longrightarrow Y^B\;}{\;B \longrightarrow Y\;} \] so the elements of \(Y^B\) must correspond exactly to the maps \(B \to Y\). This determines what \(Y^B\) “is,” just as the case \(X = 1\) of the product property told us that the elements of \(Y_0 \times Y_1\) are pairs of elements.

Families of maps, and naming schemes

A map \(f : X \times B \to Y\) (a “function of two variables”) is the same as a family of maps \(B \to Y\) parametrized by \(X\): for each element \(x : 1 \to X\) put \[ f_x : B \xrightarrow{\ \langle x \circ !_B,\, 1_B\rangle\ } X \times B \xrightarrow{\ f\ } Y, \qquad f_x(b) = f(x, b). \] Think of \(X\) as a set of names and of \(f\) as a naming scheme: \(x\) names \(f_x\). A naming scheme may or may not have the two properties

  1. every map \(B \to Y\) has at least one name (\(x \mapsto f_x\) is onto), and
  2. every nameable map has only one name (\(x \mapsto f_x\) is one-to-one).

A scheme with both properties is a universal naming scheme. We then write \(Y^B\) for the set of names, \(\mathrm{eval}: Y^B \times B \to Y\) for the scheme (“evaluation”), and \(\ulcorner f \urcorner\) for the unique name of \(f : B \to Y\): \[ \mathrm{eval}(\ulcorner f \urcorner, b) = f(b). \]

The axiom

A universal scheme compares uniquely to every scheme, and that is what we take as the axiom.

NoteAxiom: Exponentiation

For any sets \(B, Y\) there is a set \(Y^B\) and a map \(\mathrm{eval}: Y^B \times B \to Y\) such that for every \(X\) and every \(f : X \times B \to Y\) there is a unique \(\ulcorner f \urcorner : X \to Y^B\) with \[ \mathrm{eval}\circ (\ulcorner f \urcorner \times 1_B) = f . \] \[ \begin{array}{ccc} Y^B \times B & \xrightarrow{\ \mathrm{eval}\ } & Y \\[2pt] {\scriptstyle \ulcorner f \urcorner \times 1_B}\Big\uparrow & \nearrow_{\scriptstyle f} & \\[2pt] X \times B & & \end{array} \]

We call \(\ulcorner f \urcorner\) the transpose (or name, or curried form) of \(f\). Explicitly, \(\ulcorner f \urcorner(x) = \ulcorner f_x \urcorner\): the parametrized name of \(f\) assigns to \(x\) the name of \(f_x\).

Note the bootstrap again: \(\mathrm{eval}\) is the map corresponding to \(1_{Y^B}\), i.e. the unique \(f\) with \(\ulcorner f \urcorner = 1_{Y^B}\). It plays the role the projections played for products.

TipComparison with \(\mathsf{ZFC}\)

In \(\mathsf{ZFC}\) the set of functions \({}^B Y\) is constructed: it is carved out of \(\mathcal{P}(B \times Y)\) by Separation, so its existence is a consequence of Power Set. Here the direction is reversed: \(Y^B\) is primitive, characterized only by how maps into it behave, and the power set will appear below as the special case \(2^B\). Note also that the hom-set \(\mathcal{S}(B, Y)\) is a collection in the metatheory; the axiom says that it is internalized by an object of \(\mathcal{S}\), whose elements \(1 \to Y^B\) correspond to it.

Example: three descriptions of a motion

Let \(B\) be the particles of a cloud, \(E\) the points of space, \(T\) the instants of a time interval. A motion of the cloud can be given in three equivalent ways: \[ T \longrightarrow E^B, \qquad T \times B \longrightarrow E, \qquad B \longrightarrow E^T . \] The first assigns to each instant a placement of the cloud; the middle one assigns to each instant and particle a position; the last assigns to each particle its path. The passage between the outer two uses the axiom twice and the twist \(B \times T \cong T \times B\) once.

Maps into a mapping set can always be “transformed down,” but maps out of a mapping set (operators, functionals) cannot. That is why the choice of description matters:

  • center of mass is a functional \(E^B \to E\), so to get the trajectory of the center of mass we need the motion in the form \(T \to E^B\) and compose: \(T \to E^B \to E\);
  • velocity is an operator \(E^T \to V^T\), so to get the velocity field we need \(B \to E^T\), compose to get \(B \to V^T\), and transpose twice to get \(T \to V^B\) (the velocity field at each instant).

Functoriality and naturality

Exponentials are functorial in both variables

Proposition 1 (Covariance in the base (L–R 5.2))
Given \(\varphi : Y_1 \to Y_2\), there is a unique \(\varphi^B : Y_1^B \to Y_2^B\) with \[ \varphi^B \ulcorner g \urcorner = \ulcorner \varphi g \urcorner \quad \text{for all } g : B \to Y_1 . \]

NoteProof

Proof. To make a map into \(Y_2^B\), it suffices to make a map \(Y_1^B \times B \to Y_2\); take \(\varphi \circ \mathrm{eval}_1\) and set \(\varphi^B := \ulcorner \varphi\, \mathrm{eval}_1 \urcorner\), so \(\mathrm{eval}_2(\varphi^B \times 1_B) = \varphi\,\mathrm{eval}_1\). Then \[ \mathrm{eval}_2(\varphi^B\ulcorner g \urcorner \times 1_B) = \mathrm{eval}_2(\varphi^B \times 1_B)(\ulcorner g \urcorner \times 1_B) = \varphi\, \mathrm{eval}_1(\ulcorner g \urcorner \times 1_B) = \varphi g = \mathrm{eval}_2(\ulcorner \varphi g \urcorner \times 1_B), \] and the uniqueness clause of the axiom gives \(\varphi^B\ulcorner g \urcorner = \ulcorner \varphi g \urcorner\). The argument works verbatim for generalized elements \(g : X \times B \to Y_1\).

Proposition 2 (Contravariance in the exponent (L–R 5.4, 5.5))
Given \(\beta : B_2 \to B_1\), there is a unique \(Y^\beta : Y^{B_1} \to Y^{B_2}\) with \[ Y^\beta \ulcorner g \urcorner = \ulcorner g \beta \urcorner, \qquad\text{namely}\qquad Y^\beta = \ulcorner \mathrm{eval}_1 \circ (1 \times \beta) \urcorner . \] Moreover \(Y^{\beta\alpha} = Y^\alpha Y^\beta\) and \(Y^{1_B} = 1\).

NoteProof

Proof. The construction of \(Y^\beta\) is as in Proposition 1. For the composition law, evaluate both sides on a name: \(Y^\alpha Y^\beta \ulcorner g \urcorner = Y^\alpha\ulcorner g\beta \urcorner = \ulcorner (g\beta)\alpha \urcorner\) and \(Y^{\beta\alpha}\ulcorner g \urcorner = \ulcorner g(\beta\alpha) \urcorner\). These agree by associativity of composition — the proposition is “a higher expression of associativity.”

When \(\beta\) is a monomorphism, \(Y^\beta\) is restriction of maps to a part of the domain.

In the language of last time (cf. L–R Ex. 5.17):

  • \((-)^B : \mathcal{S}\to \mathcal{S}\), \(Y \mapsto Y^B\), \(\varphi \mapsto \varphi^B\) is a (covariant) functor,
  • \(Y^{(-)} : \mathcal{S}^{op} \to \mathcal{S}\), \(B \mapsto Y^B\), \(\beta \mapsto Y^\beta\) is a contravariant functor,
  • \(- \times B : \mathcal{S}\to \mathcal{S}\), \(X \mapsto X \times B\), \(\xi \mapsto \xi \times 1_B\) is a functor.

The transpose is a natural bijection

Fix \(B\). For each \(X\) and \(Y\) the axiom gives a bijection \[ \theta_{X,Y} : \mathcal{S}(X \times B,Y) \;\xrightarrow{\ \cong\ }\; \mathcal{S}(X,Y^B), \qquad f \mapsto \ulcorner f \urcorner. \]

Proposition 3 (Naturality of transposition)
For \(\xi : X' \to X\) and \(\varphi : Y \to Y'\), \[ \ulcorner f \circ (\xi \times 1_B) \urcorner = \ulcorner f \urcorner \circ \xi, \qquad \ulcorner \varphi \circ f \urcorner = \varphi^B \circ \ulcorner f \urcorner. \] That is, \(\theta\) is a natural isomorphism between the functors \(\mathcal{S}(-\times B,-)\) and \(\mathcal{S}(-,(-)^B)\) from \(\mathcal{S}^{op}\times\mathcal{S}\) to sets.

NoteProof

Proof. The second identity is Proposition 1 for generalized elements. For the first, \(\mathrm{eval}(\ulcorner f \urcorner\xi \times 1_B) = \mathrm{eval}(\ulcorner f \urcorner \times 1_B)(\xi \times 1_B) = f(\xi \times 1_B)\), so \(\ulcorner f \urcorner\xi\) satisfies the defining equation of \(\ulcorner f(\xi\times 1_B) \urcorner\); apply uniqueness.

So the crude “bar” notation of L–R means precisely: a natural isomorphism of hom-functors. Two consequences are worth stating now.

1. Representability. For fixed \(B, Y\), the contravariant functor \(X \mapsto \mathcal{S}(X \times B,Y)\) is isomorphic to \(\mathcal{S}(-,Y^B)\); one says \(Y^B\) represents it. The axiom can be read as: “this functor is representable.” (We will see in a few weeks that this pins \(Y^B\) down up to unique isomorphism — the Yoneda lemma.)

2. Adjunction. Naturality in both variables says that \(-\times B\) is left adjoint to \((-)^B\), written \(- \times B \dashv (-)^B\). This is the formal definition we will meet next week; for now, compare the two universal maps with the product case:

product \(\;Y_0 \times Y_1\) exponential \(\;Y^B\)
bijection \(\dfrac{X \to Y_0 \times Y_1}{X \to Y_0,\; X \to Y_1}\) \(\dfrac{X \to Y^B}{X \times B \to Y}\)
universal map from \(1\) above the bar projections \(p_0, p_1\) \(\mathrm{eval}: Y^B \times B \to Y\)
universal map from \(1\) below the bar diagonal \(\delta_X\) \(\eta_X = \ulcorner 1_{X\times B} \urcorner : X \to (X\times B)^B\)
downward process \(f \mapsto (p_0 f, p_1 f)\) \(g \mapsto \mathrm{eval}\circ (g \times 1_B)\)
upward process \(\langle f_0, f_1\rangle = (f_0\times f_1)\,\delta_X\) \(\ulcorner f \urcorner = f^B \circ \eta_X\)

The last entry is the second naturality identity applied to \(\varphi = f\) and the identity: \(\ulcorner f \urcorner = \ulcorner f \circ 1_{X\times B} \urcorner = f^B\ulcorner 1_{X \times B} \urcorner\). In elements, \(\eta_X(x) = \ulcorner b \mapsto \langle x, b\rangle \urcorner\). (\(\eta\) and \(\mathrm{eval}\) are the unit and counit of the adjunction.)

TipOptional: composition is itself a map (L–R 5.6)

Transposing \(Y_2^{Y_1} \times Y_1^B \times B \xrightarrow{1 \times \mathrm{eval}} Y_2^{Y_1} \times Y_1 \xrightarrow{\mathrm{eval}} Y_2\) gives \[ c : Y_2^{Y_1} \times Y_1^B \to Y_2^B, \qquad c(\ulcorner \varphi \urcorner, \ulcorner g \urcorner) = \ulcorner \varphi g \urcorner. \] Transposing once more gives \(Y_2^{Y_1} \to (Y_2^B)^{(Y_1^B)}\), \(\ulcorner \varphi \urcorner\mapsto \ulcorner \varphi^B \urcorner\): the functor \((-)^B\) is itself represented by an internal map. The operation that defines a category is internalized.

Power objects

Combine the truth-value axiom with the exponential axiom: \[ \frac{\;1 \longrightarrow 2^B\;}{\;B \longrightarrow 2\;} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{\;B \longrightarrow 2\;}{\;\text{part of } B\;}. \] So the elements of \(2^B\) are names of parts of \(B\) — \(2^B\) is the power set, now as a special case of mapping sets. The evaluation map is membership: if \(\varphi\) is the characteristic map of the part \(i\), then \[ [\, b \in i \,] := \mathrm{eval}(\ulcorner \varphi \urcorner, b) = \varphi(b) \in 2 . \] Contravariant functoriality has a familiar meaning:

Exercise 1 (L–R 5.7) For \(\beta : B_2 \to B_1\), the map \(2^\beta : 2^{B_1} \to 2^{B_2}\) represents taking inverse images along \(\beta\) of parts of \(B_1\). (Use: the inverse image of a part is a pullback, and the characteristic map of a pullback is a composite.)

TipTeaser: Cantor’s theorem without elements

Compare Cantor’s theorem in the cardinals notes. Lawvere’s fixed point theorem, which we will see later, shows: if \(g : B \times B \to Y\) is such that every map \(B \to Y\) is of the form \(g(b_0, -)\), then every \(\tau: Y \to Y\) has a fixed point. Since \(\neg : 2 \to 2\) has no fixed point, no \(B \to 2^B\) can be onto names. The diagonal argument is the bootstrap with \(\delta_B\).

The exponential laws

To justify the notation \(Y^B\) we want \(|Y^B| = |Y|^{|B|}\) for finite sets, and more generally the rules of exponent arithmetic as isomorphisms. L–R check each one by constructing explicit inverse maps and testing on elements. With naturality we can do all of them with one lemma.

Lemma 1 (Representability lemma)
If there are bijections \(\theta_T : \mathcal{S}(T,A) \cong \mathcal{S}(T,A')\) natural in \(T\), then \(a := \theta_A(1_A) : A \to A'\) is an isomorphism (and \(\theta_T = a \circ -\)). The same holds with \(\mathcal{S}(A,T) \cong \mathcal{S}(A',T)\) natural in \(T\).

NoteProof

Proof. For \(h : T \to A\), naturality applied to \(h\) gives \(\theta_T(h) = \theta_T(1_A \circ h) = \theta_A(1_A) \circ h = a h\). Let \(a' := \theta_{A'}^{-1}(1_{A'}) : A' \to A\). Then \(a a' = \theta_{A'}(a') = 1_{A'}\), and \(\theta_A(a' a) = a a' a = a = \theta_A(1_A)\), so \(a'a = 1_A\) since \(\theta_A\) is injective. This is the bootstrap once more: everything is determined by where the identity goes.

So to prove \(A \cong A'\) it suffices to give a natural bijection between maps from an arbitrary \(T\) into \(A\) and into \(A'\) — i.e., to compare generalized elements.

Proposition 4 (Exponential laws (L–R 5.8–5.10))
\[ \begin{aligned} Y^1 &\cong Y, & 1^B &\cong 1, & (Y_0 \times Y_1)^B &\cong Y_0^B \times Y_1^B,\\ Y^{X \times B} &\cong (Y^B)^X, & Y^0 &\cong 1, & Y^{A+B} &\cong Y^A \times Y^B . \end{aligned} \]

NoteProof

Proof. Each is a chain of natural bijections, followed by Lemma 1. For example: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{S}(T,(Y^B)^X) &\cong \mathcal{S}(T \times X,Y^B) \cong \mathcal{S}((T \times X) \times B,Y) \\ &\cong \mathcal{S}(T \times (X \times B),Y) \cong \mathcal{S}(T,Y^{X\times B}), \end{aligned} \] using the associativity isomorphism of products (natural in \(T\)) in the middle. Similarly \[ \mathcal{S}(T,(Y_0\times Y_1)^B) \cong \mathcal{S}(T\times B,Y_0 \times Y_1) \cong \mathcal{S}(T \times B,Y_0) \times \mathcal{S}(T\times B,Y_1) \cong \mathcal{S}(T,Y_0^B \times Y_1^B). \] \(Y^1 \cong Y\) and \(1^B \cong 1\) are the same kind of one-line computation.

The laws involving \(0\) and \(+\) need one more ingredient, which is interesting in its own right.

Theorem 1 (Exponentials force distributivity)
In any category with finite products, finite sums, and exponentials, \[ T \times 0 \cong 0, \qquad (A + B) \times T \cong A \times T + B \times T . \]

NoteProof

Proof. Now we use the contravariant version of the lemma, natural in the codomain \(Y\): \[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{S}((A+B) \times T,Y) &\cong \mathcal{S}(A+B,Y^T) \cong \mathcal{S}(A,Y^T) \times \mathcal{S}(B,Y^T) \\ &\cong \mathcal{S}(A \times T,Y) \times \mathcal{S}(B\times T,Y) \cong \mathcal{S}(A \times T + B \times T,Y). \end{aligned} \] Likewise \(\mathcal{S}(0 \times T,Y) \cong \mathcal{S}(0,Y^T)\) is a singleton for every \(Y\), so \(0 \times T\) is initial.

Distributivity does not mention exponentials, yet it follows from them (L–R return to this in §7.2): a left adjoint preserves sums, and \(-\times T\) is a left adjoint. With it, \[ \mathcal{S}(T,Y^{A+B}) \cong \mathcal{S}(T\times(A+B),Y) \cong \mathcal{S}(T\times A,Y) \times \mathcal{S}(T\times B,Y) \cong \mathcal{S}(T,Y^A \times Y^B), \] and \(\mathcal{S}(T,Y^0) \cong \mathcal{S}(T \times 0,Y) \cong \mathcal{S}(0,Y) \cong \mathcal{S}(T,1)\). This finishes Proposition 4.

Counting. Writing a finite \(B\) as \(1 + 1 + \cdots + 1\) (\(n\) summands), the laws give \(Y^B \cong Y \times \cdots \times Y\), so \(|Y^B| = |Y|^n\). In particular \(|2^B| = 2^{|B|}\).

ImportantMethodological point

L–R verify these isomorphisms by checking on elements \(1 \to Y^B\). That is legitimate in \(\mathcal{S}\) because \(1\) separates mappings — but this axiom fails in most toposes. The argument with generalized elements \(T \to Y^B\) uses only the exponential axiom and therefore works in every cartesian closed category.

Elementary toposes

We can now collect the axioms into a definition that no longer mentions \(\mathcal{S}\).

NoteDefinition: elementary topos

A category \(\mathcal{E}\) is an elementary topos if

  1. \(\mathcal{E}\) has finite limits (a terminal object and pullbacks suffice);
  2. \(\mathcal{E}\) has exponentials: for all \(B, Y\) an object \(Y^B\) with \(\mathrm{eval}: Y^B \times B \to Y\) as in the axiom (a category with 1 and 2 is called cartesian closed);
  3. \(\mathcal{E}\) has a subobject classifier: an object \(\Omega\) and a map \(\mathrm{true} : 1 \to \Omega\) such that for every mono \(m : A' \rightarrowtail A\) there is a unique \(\chi_m : A \to \Omega\) making \[ \begin{array}{ccc} A' & \longrightarrow & 1\\ {\scriptstyle m}\big\downarrow & & \big\downarrow{\scriptstyle \mathrm{true}}\\ A & \xrightarrow[\ \chi_m\ ]{} & \Omega \end{array} \] a pullback.

In \(\mathcal{S}\), \(\Omega = 2\) and condition 3 is L–R’s axiom of membership via truth values, formulated with pullbacks. Conditions 2 and 3 together give power objects \(\Omega^B\), whose elements name parts of \(B\).

Remarkably, what we did not list follows: every elementary topos also has finite colimits (initial object, sums, coequalizers). This is a nontrivial theorem (due to Paré); the distributivity argument above gives a small taste of how much the exponentials carry.

What is special about \(\mathcal{S}\)

\(\mathcal{S}\) is a topos that satisfies further axioms:

  • \(1\) separates mappings (and \(0 \not\cong 1\)): \(\mathcal{S}\) is well-pointed;
  • \(\Omega = 2 = 1 + 1\): the topos is Boolean (internal logic is classical);
  • a natural numbers object (the categorical axiom of infinity);
  • the axiom of choice: every epimorphism has a section.

These, added to the topos axioms, make up Lawvere’s Elementary Theory of the Category of Sets (ETCS), which L–R summarize in Chapter 6. Comparing with \(\mathsf{ZFC}\): Power Set and Separation are replaced by \(\Omega^B\) and the subobject classifier, Infinity by the natural numbers object, Choice by split epis — and there is no counterpart of Replacement. We take up this comparison in Week 14.

Examples and non-examples

  • \(\mathcal{S}/X\) (L–R Ex. 5.12): an object is an \(X\)-indexed family \((A_x)_{x\in X}\); exponentials are computed fiberwise, \((Y^B)_x = Y_x^{B_x}\). A topos — “sets varying over a discrete parameter.”
  • \(G\)-sets for a group \(G\) (Ex. 5.13): \(Y^X\) is the set of all maps \(X \to Y\) with \((g\cdot f)(x) = g\, f(g^{-1}x)\); the inverse is essential. A topos, with \(\Omega = 2\) (trivial action).
  • \(M\)-sets for a monoid \(M\): the exponential is subtler, and \(\Omega\) is the set of left ideals of \(M\) — e.g. for \(M = (\mathbb{N}, +)\), “sets evolving in discrete time,” \(\Omega\) is infinite. A topos that is not Boolean: here condition 3 holds but \(\Omega \neq 1+1\).
  • Posets (Ex. 5.14): cartesian closed (\(Y^X\) = monotone maps, ordered pointwise), but not a topos: the identity map from a two-element discrete poset to a two-element chain is mono and epi but not iso, which cannot happen in a topos.
  • Finite-dimensional vector spaces with \(\times = \oplus\) (Ex. 5.11): not cartesian closed. If \(L(B,Y)\) were an exponential, then \(\dim \mathrm{Hom}(X \oplus B, Y) = (x + b)y\) would equal \(\dim \mathrm{Hom}(X, L(B,Y)) = xby\) for all dimensions. (The correct partner of \(L(B, Y)\) is \(\otimes\), not \(\oplus\).)
