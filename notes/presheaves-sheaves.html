Presheaves and Sheaves: A First Look

Using the open sets of a space as a guide

Author
Affiliation

Jan Reimann

Penn State — Math 558

The idea in two sentences. A presheaf is data defined on the open sets of a space that can be restricted to smaller open sets. A sheaf is a presheaf whose data are determined by, and can be glued from, local pieces.

This handout makes these two sentences precise, tests them on examples, and then explains how sheaves form a topos whose truth values are open sets.

Step 1: the poset of open sets as a category

Fix a topological space \(X\) and let \(\mathcal{O}(X)\) be the set of its open subsets. Make \(\mathcal{O}(X)\) into a category:

  • the objects are the open sets \(U \subseteq X\);
  • there is exactly one arrow \(V \to U\) if \(V \subseteq U\), and none otherwise.

Identities exist because \(U \subseteq U\), and composites exist because \(\subseteq\) is transitive. Associativity and the identity laws hold automatically, since there is at most one arrow between any two objects. (Every poset is a category in this way.)

Step 2: presheaves — data that can be restricted

The model example

For each open set \(U \subseteq X\) let \[ F(U) = C(U, \mathbb{R}), \] the set of continuous real-valued functions on \(U\). If \(V \subseteq U\), any \(f\) on \(U\) can be restricted to \(f|_V\) on \(V\). So we get maps \[ F(U) \to F(V), \qquad f \mapsto f|_V \qquad (V \subseteq U), \] and restriction behaves as expected: \[ f|_U = f, \qquad (f|_V)|_W = f|_W \quad \text{for } W \subseteq V \subseteq U. \] That is the whole definition, abstracted.

NoteDefinition: presheaf

A presheaf \(F\) on \(X\) assigns

  • to each open set \(U\) a set \(F(U)\), whose elements are called sections over \(U\), and
  • to each pair \(V \subseteq U\) a restriction map \(F(U) \to F(V)\), \(s \mapsto s|_V\),

such that \(s|_U = s\) and \((s|_V)|_W = s|_W\) whenever \(W \subseteq V \subseteq U\). The elements of \(F(X)\) are called global sections.

In categorical language: a presheaf is a functor \(F : \mathcal{O}(X)^{op} \to \mathcal{S}\). The two rules are exactly preservation of identities and of composition.

TipWhy “op”?

The arrow \(V \to U\) goes from small to big, but restriction goes from big to small. Intuitively, a bigger open set imposes more constraints, so there are fewer sections over it; you can always pass from a section over a big set to one over a small set, but not back.

More examples

In each case the restriction maps are the obvious ones.

  1. Other function spaces: all functions \(U \to \mathbb{R}\); the \(C^1\) functions; the bounded continuous functions.
  2. The constant presheaf: \(F(U) = S\) for a fixed set \(S\), with every restriction map the identity.
  3. Open sets: \(\Omega(U) = \{ W : W \subseteq U \text{ open} \}\), with restriction \(W|_V = W \cap V\).
  4. Sections of a map: for a continuous \(p : E \to X\), let \(F(U) = \{ s : U \to E \text{ continuous} \mid p \circ s = \mathrm{id}_U \}\).
  5. Square roots (a special case of 4): take \(X = \mathbb{C}\setminus \{0\}\), and let \(F(U)\) be the set of continuous \(g : U \to \mathbb{C}\) with \(g(z)^2 = z\) for all \(z \in U\).

Morphisms of presheaves

NoteDefinition: morphism of presheaves

A morphism \(\varphi : F \to G\) is a family of functions \(\varphi_U : F(U) \to G(U)\), one for each open \(U\), that commutes with restriction: \[ \varphi_U(s)|_V = \varphi_V(s|_V) \qquad (s \in F(U),\ V \subseteq U). \]

This is exactly a natural transformation between the functors \(F\) and \(G\).

Example. Differentiation \(C^1(U) \to C^0(U)\), \(f \mapsto f'\), is a morphism of presheaves: differentiating and then restricting is the same as restricting and then differentiating.

Step 3: the gluing question — from presheaves to sheaves

Suppose an open set is covered by smaller ones, \(U = \bigcup_i U_i\), and we are given sections \(s_i \in F(U_i)\) that agree on overlaps: \[ s_i|_{U_i \cap U_j} = s_j|_{U_i \cap U_j} \qquad \text{for all } i, j. \] Such a family is called compatible. Is there exactly one \(s \in F(U)\) with \(s|_{U_i} = s_i\) for all \(i\)?

NoteDefinition: sheaf

A presheaf \(F\) is a sheaf if for every open cover \(U = \bigcup_i U_i\) and every compatible family \(s_i \in F(U_i)\) there is a unique \(s \in F(U)\) with \(s|_{U_i} = s_i\) for all \(i\).

  • Existence of \(s\) is the gluing condition.
  • Uniqueness of \(s\) is the locality condition: a section is determined by its local pieces.

A small but useful consequence: the empty set is covered by the empty family, which is vacuously compatible. So a sheaf always has exactly one section over \(\varnothing\).

Testing the examples

The best way to absorb the definition is to test it.

  • Continuous functions form a sheaf. Functions that agree on overlaps define a unique function on the union, and continuity is a local property, so the glued function is continuous. The same argument works for all functions, for \(C^1\) functions, and for open sets (\(\Omega\)).

  • Bounded functions do not. Cover \(\mathbb{R}\) by \(U_n = (n-1, n+1)\), \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\), and let \(s_n\) be the identity function on \(U_n\). Each \(s_n\) is bounded and they agree on overlaps, but the glued function \(x \mapsto x\) is unbounded. Boundedness is a global property, so gluing fails.

  • The constant presheaf does not. Take \(U = (0,1) \cup (2,3)\) and choose \(a \neq b\) in \(S\) on the two pieces. They agree on the (empty) overlap, but no single element of \(F(U) = S\) restricts to both. It also has the wrong value \(F(\varnothing) = S\). The repaired version is the sheaf of locally constant functions \(U \to S\).

  • Square roots form a sheaf — with no global section. Gluing holds, as for continuous functions. On any slit plane, e.g. \(\mathbb{C}\setminus (-\infty, 0]\), there are exactly two continuous square roots. But on all of \(X = \mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}\) there is none: \(F(X) = \varnothing\).

Why there is no global square root

Follow a continuous square root once around the origin. As \(z = e^{i\theta}\) runs through \(\theta \in [0, 2\pi]\), a continuous choice of square root must be \(g = e^{i\theta/2}\), which travels only half way around: it starts at \(1\) and ends at \(-1\).

In sheaf terms: cover \(X\) by the two slit planes \[ U_1 = \mathbb{C}\setminus (-\infty, 0], \qquad U_2 = \mathbb{C}\setminus [0, \infty). \] Their intersection is the upper half plane together with the lower half plane. If two square roots \(g_1\) on \(U_1\) and \(g_2\) on \(U_2\) agree on the upper half plane, then \(g_2 = -g_1\) on the lower half plane (this is the picture above). So no choice of local square roots is compatible, and there is nothing to glue. The sheaf axiom is not violated; there simply is no compatible family.

Connection to toposes. A map \(1 \to F\) from the terminal sheaf \(1(U) = \{*\}\) is the same as a global section (Exercise 19.1). So the square-root sheaf is an object that is locally nonempty everywhere but has no elements at all. This is the same phenomenon that made \(\mathcal{S}/X\) fail to be well-pointed — now with a topological reason behind it.

Step 4: discrete spaces and the slice \(\mathcal{S}/X\)

If \(X\) is discrete, every subset is open, and every \(U\) is covered by its singletons, which are pairwise disjoint. A compatible family on this cover is any choice of sections \(s_x \in F(\{x\})\), since all overlaps are empty. So the sheaf condition says \[ F(U) \;\cong\; \prod_{x \in U} F(\{x\}), \] and a sheaf is nothing but the family of sets \(\big(F(\{x\})\big)_{x \in X}\). That is precisely an object of \(\mathcal{S}/X\), with fibers \(A_x = F(\{x\})\): \[ \mathrm{Sh}(X_{\text{discrete}}) \;\simeq\; \mathcal{S}/X . \] For a general space, a sheaf is still a family of sets indexed by the points (its stalks), but now the family varies continuously. In fact every sheaf is the sheaf of sections of a suitable map \(p : E \to X\) (a local homeomorphism), just as in the square-root example. Sheaves are slices with topology.

Step 5: parts and truth values

NoteDefinition: subsheaf

A subsheaf \(A \subseteq F\) is a choice of subsets \(A(U) \subseteq F(U)\) that is closed under restriction (\(s \in A(U) \Rightarrow s|_V \in A(V)\)) and is itself a sheaf.

Example. Inside \(F = C(-, \mathbb{R})\) take \(A(U) = \{ f \in C(U, \mathbb{R}) : f > 0 \text{ on } U \}\).

Now take a section \(s \in F(U)\) and ask: is \(s\) in \(A\)? In \(\mathcal{S}\) the answer is yes or no. Here the honest answer is “on part of \(U\).” Among the open sets \(W \subseteq U\), some satisfy \(s|_W \in A(W)\). By gluing, \(s\) restricted to their union is in \(A\) as well, so there is a largest such open set. That open set is the truth value: \[ [\![\, s \in A \,]\!] \;=\; \bigcup \{ W \subseteq U \text{ open} : s|_W \in A(W) \}. \] For \(s = x\) on \(\mathbb{R}\) and \(A\) the positive functions, \([\![\, x \in A \,]\!] = (0, \infty)\).

So the truth-value object of the topos \(\mathrm{Sh}(X)\) is exactly example 3 from Step 2: \[ \Omega(U) = \{ W \subseteq U \text{ open} \}, \qquad \mathrm{true}_U = U, \] and the characteristic map of \(A \subseteq F\) sends \(s \in F(U)\) to \([\![\, s \in A \,]\!]\).

Where excluded middle goes

Truth values are combined like open sets: \[ [\![\varphi \wedge \psi]\!] = [\![\varphi]\!] \cap [\![\psi]\!], \qquad [\![\varphi \vee \psi]\!] = [\![\varphi]\!] \cup [\![\psi]\!], \qquad [\![\neg \varphi]\!] = \operatorname{int}\big(X \setminus [\![\varphi]\!]\big). \] Negation must give an open set, the largest one disjoint from \([\![\varphi]\!]\), hence the interior. Therefore \[ [\![\, x > 0 \;\vee\; \neg(x > 0) \,]\!] = (0, \infty) \cup \operatorname{int}\big((-\infty, 0]\big) = \mathbb{R}\setminus \{0\} \;\neq\; \mathbb{R}. \] Excluded middle fails exactly at the boundary point \(0\), where neither “\(x > 0\)” nor its negation holds on any neighborhood.

TipPresheaves versus sheaves: sieves

For mere presheaves on \(\mathcal{O}(X)\), the natural truth value of “\(s \in A\)” is the whole collection \[ \{ W \subseteq U \text{ open} : s|_W \in A(W) \}. \] This collection is closed under shrinking — a sieve on \(U\) — and it need not be determined by its union. For example, the sieve generated by \((-\infty, 0)\) and \((0, \infty)\) (all opens contained in one of them) differs from the sieve of all open subsets of \(\mathbb{R}\setminus \{0\}\), although both have union \(\mathbb{R}\setminus \{0\}\). The sheaf condition is exactly what collapses a sieve to its union, which is why sheaves have the simpler \(\Omega\).

Step 6: the general picture

Nothing in the definition of a presheaf used that \(\mathcal{O}(X)\) comes from a space. Replace it by any small category \(C\) and you get presheaves \(C^{op} \to \mathcal{S}\): data indexed by the objects of \(C\), restrictable along the arrows of \(C\). The examples from lecture are all of this kind:

index category \(C\) presheaves on \(C\)
\(\mathcal{O}(X)\) presheaves on the space \(X\)
a monoid \(M\) (one object) \(M\)-sets
a group \(G\) (one object) \(G\)-sets
\(V \rightrightarrows E\) directed graphs
one arrow \(1 \to 0\) the Sierpiński topos

The sheaf condition needs extra structure: a specification of which families of arrows “cover” an object. A category with such a specification is a site, and the categories of sheaves on sites are the Grothendieck toposes.

Exercises

Exercise 19.1 Let \(1\) be the presheaf with \(1(U) = \{*\}\) for all \(U\). Show that morphisms \(1 \to F\) correspond bijectively to global sections \(F(X)\). (Hint: \(X\) is the largest element of \(\mathcal{O}(X)\).)

Exercise 19.2 Show that \(\Omega(U) = \{ W \subseteq U \text{ open}\}\), with \(W|_V = W \cap V\), is a sheaf. Then show that the subsheaves of \(1\) correspond exactly to the open sets of \(X\).

Exercise 19.3 Let \(F = C(-, \mathbb{R})\) and \(A(U) = \{ f : f \geq 0 \text{ on } U\}\). Show that \(A\) is a subsheaf, and compute \([\![\, x \in A \,]\!]\) for \(x\) the identity function on \(\mathbb{R}\). Why is the answer not \([0, \infty)\)?

Exercise 19.4 Show that the presheaf of locally constant functions \(U \to S\) is a sheaf. Describe its sections over \(U = (0,1) \cup (2,3)\), and over a connected open set.

Exercise 19.5 The Sierpiński space has points \(\{a, b\}\) and open sets \(\varnothing\), \(\{b\}\), \(\{a, b\}\). Show that a sheaf on it amounts to a single map \(F(\{a, b\}) \to F(\{b\})\), and that \(\Omega\) is the map \(\{\varnothing, \{b\}, \{a,b\}\} \to \{\varnothing, \{b\}\}\), \(W \mapsto W \cap \{b\}\). Compare with the Sierpiński topos from lecture.
