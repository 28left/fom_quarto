Cofinality and Inaccessible Cardinals

Cofinality

Recall two basic facts about cardinalities:

  • Finite unions of finite sets are finite.
  • Countable unions of countable sets are countable (using \(\mathsf{AC}\)).

This means it is “hard” to reach the cardinals \(\aleph_0\) and \(\aleph_1\) “from below”: you cannot obtain a set of size \(\aleph_0\) by putting together a few small pieces, and similarly for \(\aleph_1\). The notion of cofinality quantifies “how hard” it is to reach an ordinal from below.

Throughout this section, \({}\alpha\) denotes a limit ordinal (following Kunen, Def. I.13.10, we define cofinality only for limit ordinals).

Definition 1
A set \(X \subseteq \alpha\) is cofinal in \({}\alpha\) if \[ \forall \gamma < \alpha \; \exists \xi \in X \;\; \gamma < \xi. \] Equivalently, \[ \alpha = \bigcup_{\xi \in X} \xi = \sup X. \]

Definition 2
The cofinality of \({}\alpha\) is \[ \operatorname{cf}(\alpha) = \text{least ordinal } \beta \text{ such that there exists } f: \beta \to \alpha \text{ with } \operatorname{ran}(f) \text{ cofinal in } \alpha. \] We call such an \(f\) a cofinal map.

We can always assume that a cofinal map \(f: \operatorname{cf}(\alpha) \to \alpha\) is strictly increasing: if necessary, we can “thin it out”.

Lemma 1
There exists a strictly increasing cofinal map \(g: \operatorname{cf}(\alpha) \to \alpha\).

NoteProof

Proof. Let \(f: \operatorname{cf}(\alpha) \to \alpha\) be cofinal. Define \(g\) by recursion: \[ g(\eta) = \max\left\{ f(\eta), \; \sup\{g(\zeta)+1 \colon \zeta < \eta\} \right\}. \] We have to check that \(g(\eta) < \alpha\). If \(\sup\{g(\zeta)+1 \colon \zeta < \eta\} = \alpha\), then \(g\mid_{\eta}\) would be a cofinal map from \(\eta < \operatorname{cf}(\alpha)\) into \({}\alpha\) (here we use that \({}\alpha\) is a limit), contradicting the minimality of \(\operatorname{cf}(\alpha)\). By construction, \(g\) is strictly increasing, and \(g(\eta) \geq f(\eta)\) implies that \(g\) is cofinal.

Example 1

  • \(\operatorname{cf}(\omega) = \omega\)
  • \(\operatorname{cf}(\omega+\omega) = \omega\), via \(n \mapsto \omega + n\)
  • \(\operatorname{cf}(\omega\cdot\omega) = \omega\), via \(n \mapsto \omega \cdot n\)

Proposition 1
For any limit ordinal \({}\alpha\),

  1. \(\operatorname{cf}(\alpha) \leq \alpha\),
  2. \(\operatorname{cf}(\alpha)\) is a cardinal,
  3. \(\operatorname{cf}(\operatorname{cf}(\alpha)) = \operatorname{cf}(\alpha)\).
NoteProof

Proof.

  1. The identity map \(\alpha \to \alpha\) is cofinal.

  2. Let \(\beta = \operatorname{cf}(\alpha)\) and suppose \(|\beta| < \beta\). Let \(h: |\beta| \to \beta\) be a bijection and \(f: \beta \to \alpha\) cofinal. Then \(f \circ h: |\beta| \to \alpha\) has the same range as \(f\) and hence is cofinal, contradicting the minimality of \({}\beta\).

  3. By (1), \(\operatorname{cf}(\operatorname{cf}(\alpha)) \leq \operatorname{cf}(\alpha)\). For the other inequality, let \(f: \operatorname{cf}(\alpha) \to \alpha\) be strictly increasing and cofinal (Lemma 1), and let \(g: \delta \to \operatorname{cf}(\alpha)\) be cofinal. Then \(f \circ g: \delta \to \alpha\) is cofinal: given \(\gamma < \alpha\), pick \(\eta\) with \(f(\eta) > \gamma\), and \(\zeta < \delta\) with \(g(\zeta) > \eta\). Then \(f(g(\zeta)) > f(\eta) > \gamma\). Hence \(\delta \geq \operatorname{cf}(\alpha)\).

Regular and singular cardinals

Recall the enumeration of all infinite cardinals by means of the \(\aleph\)-sequence:

\[\begin{equation*} \aleph_0 = \omega, \quad \aleph_{\alpha+1} = \aleph_\alpha^+, \quad \aleph_\lambda = \sup \{ \aleph_\xi \colon \xi<\lambda\} \; \text{ for limit } \lambda. \end{equation*}\]

Here \(\kappa^+\) is the least cardinal \(> \kappa\). Some cardinals are limits of short sequences of smaller cardinals – for example, \[\begin{equation*} \aleph_\omega = \lim_n \aleph_n \end{equation*}\] is uncountable, but a limit of a countable sequence of smaller cardinals.

Definition 3
Let \({}\kappa\) be an infinite cardinal. \({}\kappa\) is

  • regular if \(\operatorname{cf}(\kappa) = \kappa\),
  • singular if \(\operatorname{cf}(\kappa) < \kappa\).

In other words, a regular cardinal \({}\kappa\) cannot be reached by less than \({}\kappa\)-many steps: \[\begin{equation*} \operatorname{reg}(\kappa) \iff \forall \alpha < \kappa \; \forall f \;( f: \alpha \to \kappa \; \to \; \sup_{\xi < \alpha} f(\xi) < \kappa). \end{equation*}\]

Example 2

  • \(\aleph_0\) is regular.
  • \(\aleph_1\) is regular.
  • \(\aleph_\omega\) is singular, with \(\operatorname{cf}(\aleph_\omega) = \omega\), as witnessed by the cofinal sequence \(\aleph_0, \aleph_1, \aleph_2, \ldots \to \aleph_\omega\).

The regularity of \(\aleph_1\) is an instance of a general fact.

Proposition 2
(\(\mathsf{AC}\)) Every infinite successor cardinal is regular.

NoteProof

Proof. Suppose \(\delta = \operatorname{cf}(\aleph_{\alpha+1}) < \aleph_{\alpha+1}\). Then there exists a cofinal \(f: \delta \to \aleph_{\alpha+1}\), so \[ \aleph_{\alpha+1} = \bigcup_{\gamma < \delta} f(\gamma). \] Now \(|\delta| < \aleph_{\alpha+1}\), and \(|f(\gamma)| < \aleph_{\alpha+1}\) for all \(\gamma < \delta\). Hence \(|\delta| \leq \aleph_\alpha\) and \(|f(\gamma)| \leq \aleph_\alpha\) for all \(\gamma < \delta\). This implies \[ |\aleph_{\alpha+1}| = \left|\bigcup_{\gamma<\delta} f(\gamma)\right| \leq \aleph_\alpha \cdot \aleph_\alpha = \aleph_\alpha, \] a consequence of Hessenberg’s Theorem (see the notes on Cardinals). This is a contradiction.

On the other hand, \[\begin{equation*} \aleph_\omega, \aleph_{\omega+\omega}, \aleph_{\aleph_{\omega}}, \aleph_{\aleph_{\aleph_\omega}}, \ldots \end{equation*}\] are all singular. This follows from the following result, which reduces the cofinality of \(\aleph_\lambda\) to that of its index.

Proposition 3
If \({}\lambda\) is a limit ordinal, then \(\operatorname{cf}(\aleph_\lambda) = \operatorname{cf}(\lambda)\).

NoteProof

Proof. (\(\leq\)) Let \(f: \gamma \to \lambda\) be cofinal. Then \[ \hat{f}: \gamma \to \aleph_\lambda, \quad \beta \mapsto \aleph_{f(\beta)} \] is cofinal in \(\aleph_\lambda\): If \(\eta < \aleph_\lambda\), then \(\eta < \aleph_\xi\) for some \(\xi < \lambda\). Pick \(\beta\) with \(f(\beta) > \xi\). Then \(\aleph_{f(\beta)} > \aleph_\xi > \eta\).

(\(\geq\)) Let \(f: \gamma \to \aleph_\lambda\) be cofinal. We turn \(f\) into a cofinal map \(g: \gamma \to \lambda\) by letting \[ g(\beta) = \text{the } \alpha \text{ such that } |f(\beta)| = \aleph_\alpha \] (and \(g(\beta) = 0\) if \(f(\beta)\) is finite). Since \(f(\beta) < \aleph_\lambda\), \(g(\beta) < \lambda\). If \(\xi < \lambda\), pick \(\beta\) with \(f(\beta) > \aleph_\xi\). Then \(|f(\beta)| \geq \aleph_\xi\), so \(g(\beta) \geq \xi\). Hence \(g\) is cofinal in \({}\lambda\).

For example, \(\operatorname{cf}(\aleph_{\omega+\omega}) = \operatorname{cf}(\omega+\omega) = \omega\).

Cofinality of the continuum

Cofinality also puts a restriction on the possible values of \(2^\kappa\). To prove this, we need a fundamental inequality between cardinal sums and products. For a family of cardinals \((\kappa_i)_{i\in I}\), let \[ \sum_{i \in I} \kappa_i = \left|\bigcup_{i \in I} \{i\} \times \kappa_i \right|, \qquad \prod_{i \in I} \kappa_i = \left| \left\{ g \colon g \text{ function}, \; \operatorname{dom}(g) = I, \; \forall i \; g(i) \in \kappa_i \right\}\right|. \]

Theorem 1 (König’s Theorem)
(\(\mathsf{AC}\)) Let \((\kappa_i)_{i\in I}\) and \((\lambda_i)_{i\in I}\) be families of cardinals such that \(\kappa_i < \lambda_i\) for all \(i \in I\). Then \[ \sum_{i \in I} \kappa_i < \prod_{i\in I} \lambda_i. \]

NoteProof

Proof. The map \((i,\xi) \mapsto g_{i,\xi}\), where \(g_{i,\xi}(i) = \xi+1\) and \(g_{i,\xi}(j) = 0\) for \(j \neq i\), is an injection, so \(\sum \kappa_i \leq \prod \lambda_i\).

Suppose \(f: \bigcup_{i} \{i\}\times \kappa_i \to \prod_i \lambda_i\). We show that \(f\) is not onto. For each \(i \in I\), the set \[ \{ f(i,\xi)(i) \colon \xi < \kappa_i\} \subseteq \lambda_i \] has cardinality \(\leq \kappa_i < \lambda_i\), so we can let \(y(i)\) be the least element of \(\lambda_i\) not in this set. Then \(y \in \prod_i \lambda_i\), but \(y \neq f(i,\xi)\) for all \(i, \xi\), since \(y\) and \(f(i,\xi)\) differ at coordinate \(i\).

(The Axiom of Choice is needed to ensure that the sets on both sides are well-orderable, so that their cardinalities are defined; for families of arbitrary sets, one uses \(\mathsf{AC}\) to choose the witnesses \(y(i)\).)

Proposition 4
If \(\kappa \geq \aleph_0\), then \(\operatorname{cf}(2^\kappa) > \kappa\).

NoteProof

Proof. Suppose \(f: \delta \to 2^\kappa\) is cofinal with \(\delta \leq \kappa\), so that \[ 2^\kappa = \bigcup_{\gamma < \delta} f(\gamma). \] Then \(|f(\gamma)| < 2^\kappa\) for all \(\gamma < \delta\), and by König’s Theorem, \[\begin{align*} 2^\kappa = \left|\bigcup_{\gamma<\delta} f(\gamma)\right| & \leq \sum_{\gamma < \delta} |f(\gamma)| \\ & < \prod_{\gamma < \delta} 2^\kappa = (2^\kappa)^\delta = 2^{\kappa \cdot \delta} = 2^\kappa, \end{align*}\] where the last equality uses \(\delta \leq \kappa\). This is a contradiction.

Corollary 1
\(2^{\aleph_0} \neq \aleph_\omega\).

NoteProof

Proof. \(\operatorname{cf}(2^{\aleph_0}) > \aleph_0 = \operatorname{cf}(\aleph_\omega)\).

Inaccessible cardinals

We have seen that successor cardinals are regular, and that \(\aleph_\lambda\) for “small” limits \({}\lambda\) are singular. This suggests the question:

TipQuestion

Are there regular limit cardinals, i.e. regular cardinals of the form \(\aleph_\lambda\) with \(\lambda\) limit?

If \(\aleph_\lambda\) is a regular limit cardinal, then by Proposition 3 and Proposition 1, \[ \aleph_\lambda = \operatorname{cf}(\aleph_\lambda) = \operatorname{cf}(\lambda) \leq \lambda, \] and since always \(\lambda \leq \aleph_\lambda\), we must have \(\aleph_\lambda = \lambda\). So a regular limit cardinal must be very large: it is a fixed point of the \(\aleph\)-function.

This is captured by the notion of inaccessibility.

Definition 4 (Hausdorff 1908, Tarski, Zermelo 1930)
An uncountable cardinal \(\kappa > \omega\) is

  • weakly inaccessible if \[\begin{align*} \operatorname{reg}(\kappa) \; & \wedge \; \exists \lambda (\operatorname{lim}(\lambda) \; \wedge\; \kappa = \aleph_\lambda)\\ (& \Leftrightarrow \operatorname{reg}(\kappa) \; \wedge \; \forall \alpha < \kappa \; \, \alpha^+ < \kappa) \end{align*}\]

  • (strongly) inaccessible if \[\begin{equation*} \operatorname{reg}(\kappa) \; \wedge \; \forall \alpha < \kappa \;\; 2^{\alpha} < \kappa \end{equation*}\]

Every strongly inaccessible cardinal is weakly inaccessible, since \(\alpha^+ \leq 2^\alpha\). Under the Generalized Continuum Hypothesis,

  \((\mathsf{GCH}) \quad \forall \alpha \;\; 2^{\aleph_\alpha} = \aleph_{\alpha}^+\)

weakly and strongly inaccessible cardinals coincide.

If \(\kappa\) is (weakly) inaccessible, then \(\kappa = \aleph_\kappa\), as shown above.

Inaccessibles and the von Neumann hierarchy

The cardinality of \(V_\alpha\) grows rather fast relative to \({}\alpha\). For example,

\[ |V_{\omega+\alpha}| = \beth_\alpha \]
where the beth function \(\beth_\alpha\) is defined as \[\begin{gather*} \beth_0 = \aleph_0 \\ \beth_{\alpha+1} = 2^{\beth_\alpha} \\ \beth_{\lambda} = \sup \{ \beth_\alpha \colon \alpha < \lambda\} \quad \text{ $\lambda$ limit} \end{gather*}\]

The existence of inaccessible cardinals ensures that the von Neumann hierarchy is “long enough” for \({}\alpha\) to eventually “catch up” with the cardinality of \(V_\alpha\).

Proposition 5
If \({}\kappa\) is strongly inaccessible, then \(|V_\kappa| = \kappa\).

NoteProof

Proof. Since \(\kappa \subseteq V_\kappa\), \(|V_\kappa| \geq \kappa\). For the other inequality, it suffices to show that \(|V_\alpha| < \kappa\) for all \(\alpha < \kappa\), since then \(|V_\kappa| = |\bigcup_{\alpha<\kappa} V_\alpha| \leq \kappa \cdot \kappa = \kappa\).

We show \(|V_\alpha| < \kappa\) by induction on \(\alpha < \kappa\): if \(|V_\alpha| < \kappa\), then \(|V_{\alpha+1}| = 2^{|V_\alpha|} < \kappa\) since \({}\kappa\) is strongly inaccessible. If \(\lambda < \kappa\) is a limit, then \(V_\lambda\) is a union of \(|\lambda| < \kappa\) many sets, each of size \(< \kappa\), so by regularity of \({}\kappa\), \(|V_\lambda| < \kappa\).

This in turn implies we can bound the cardinality of elements of \(V_\kappa\).

Proposition 6
Suppose \({}\kappa\) is strongly inaccessible and \(x\subseteq V_\kappa\). Then

\[ x \in V_\kappa \; \Leftrightarrow \; |x| < \kappa. \]

NoteProof

Proof. (\(\Rightarrow\)) If \(x \in V_\kappa\), then \(x \in V_\alpha\) for some \(\alpha < \kappa\), and by transitivity \(x \subseteq V_\alpha\). By the proof of Proposition 5, \(|x| \leq |V_\alpha| < \kappa\).

(\(\Leftarrow\)) Since \(x \subseteq V_\kappa\), each \(y \in x\) has rank \(< \kappa\). Since \(|x| < \kappa\), by regularity of \({}\kappa\),

\[ \operatorname{rank}(x) = \sup\{\operatorname{rank}(y)+1 \colon y \in x\} < \kappa \]

which implies \(x \in V_\kappa\).

We have already seen that for limit \(\alpha > \omega\), \(V_\alpha\) is a model of all \(\mathsf{ZFC}\) axioms except Replacement.

Theorem 2
If \({}\kappa\) is strongly inaccessible, then \(V_\kappa \models \mathsf{ZFC}\).

NoteProof

Proof. We verify that \(V_\kappa\) satisfies the axiom of Replacement. Suppose \(x \in V_\kappa\) and \(f:x \to V_\kappa\) is a function. Then \(f[x] \subseteq V_\kappa\), and by Proposition 6, \(|f[x]| \leq |x| < \kappa\). Applying the other direction of Proposition 6 to \(f[x]\), we obtain \(f[x] \in V_\kappa\), as desired.

Suppose an inaccessible cardinal exists, and let \({}\kappa\) be the least inaccessible.
It is not hard to verify that

\[ V_\kappa \models \mathsf{ZFC}+ \text{``there does not exist an inaccessible cardinal''}. \]

(You verify that being an inaccessible cardinal is absolute for \(V_\kappa\).) Therefore, the existence of an inaccessible cardinal is not provable from \(\mathsf{ZFC}\). This fact also follows from Gödel’s second incompleteness theorem.
