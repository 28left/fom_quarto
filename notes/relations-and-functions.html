Relations and Functions

Relations, functions, Cartesian products via Replacement, isomorphism, quotients — and the object-theory/metatheory split

Author
Affiliation

Jan Reimann

Penn State — Math 558

You have used relations (orderings) and functions all your life; the point here is to say what they are inside axiomatic set theory, and to notice where an extra axiom (Replacement) is needed to build them.

Relations

Definition (binary relation). \(R\) is a (binary) relation iff \(R\) is a set of ordered pairs:

\[ \forall u \in R\;\exists x, y\;\bigl(u = \langle x, y\rangle\bigr). \]

Here \(\forall u \in R\,(\dots)\) is shorthand for \(\forall u\,(u \in R \rightarrow \dots)\). We write

\[ x \mathbin{R} y \quad\text{for}\quad \langle x, y\rangle \in R, \qquad\qquad x \mathbin{\not\mathrel{R}} y \quad\text{for}\quad \langle x, y\rangle \notin R. \]

The abbreviations \(x \mathbin{R} y\) and \(x \mathbin{\not\mathrel{R}} y\) are meaningful even when \(R\) is not a relation — we will use this later.

Usual terminology

Fix a set \(A\). The following say what it means for \(R\) to have a property on \(A\); each is just an abbreviation, obtained by plugging \(R\) into a formula.

  • transitive: \(x \mathbin{R} y \wedge y \mathbin{R} z \rightarrow x \mathbin{R} z\)
  • reflexive / irreflexive: \(\forall x \in A\;(x \mathbin{R} x)\) / \(\forall x \in A\;(x \mathbin{\not\mathrel{R}} x)\)
  • trichotomy: \(\forall x, y \in A\;\bigl(x \mathbin{R} y \vee y \mathbin{R} x \vee x = y\bigr)\)
  • symmetric: \(x \mathbin{R} y \leftrightarrow y \mathbin{R} x\)

and the three composite notions we care about:

\(R\) (strictly) partially orders \(A\)
\(R\) is transitive and irreflexive on \(A\).
\(R\) (strictly) totally orders \(A\)
\(R\) is transitive, irreflexive, and satisfies trichotomy on \(A\).
\(R\) is an equivalence relation on \(A\)
\(R\) is transitive, reflexive, and symmetric on \(A\).

We take the strict \(<\) as basic and read \(x \le y\) as \(x < y \vee x = y\). For example, \(<\) on \(\mathbb{Q}\) is a strict total order; \(\le\) is not (it is not irreflexive).

Domain, range, restriction

\(\operatorname{dom}(R) := \{\,x : \exists y\,(\langle x, y\rangle \in R)\,\}\)

\(\operatorname{ran}(R) := \{\,y : \exists x\,(\langle x, y\rangle \in R)\,\}\)

\(R \restriction A := \{\,\langle x, y\rangle \in R : x \in A\,\}\)

The definitions are stated for an arbitrary set \(R\), not only for relations. The restriction \(R \restriction A\) is used mostly for functions.

Justification (these classes are sets). Recall \(\langle x, y\rangle = \bigl\{\{x\}, \{x, y\}\bigr\}\). So if \(\langle x, y\rangle \in R\), then \(\{x\}\) and \(\{x, y\}\) lie in \(\bigcup R\), and hence \(x, y \in \bigcup\bigcup R\). Comprehension then gives

\[ \operatorname{dom}(R) = \bigl\{\,x \in \textstyle\bigcup\bigcup R : \exists y\,(\langle x, y\rangle \in R)\,\bigr\}, \]

and similarly for \(\operatorname{ran}(R)\).

Functions

Definition (function). \(R\) is a function iff \(R\) is a relation and for every \(x \in \operatorname{dom}(R)\) there is a unique \(y\) with \(\langle x, y\rangle \in R\); that unique \(y\) is written \(R(x)\).

\(F : A \to B\):   \(F\) is a function with \(\operatorname{dom}(F) = A\) and \(\operatorname{ran}(F) \subseteq B\).

\(F : A \xrightarrow[\text{onto}]{} B\):   \(F : A \to B\) and \(\operatorname{ran}(F) = B\) (a surjection).

\(F : A \xrightarrow{1\text{-}1} B\):   \(F : A \to B\) and \(\forall x, x' \in A\;\bigl(F(x) = F(x') \rightarrow x = x'\bigr)\) (an injection).

\(F : A \xrightarrow[\text{onto}]{1\text{-}1} B\):   both of the above (a bijection from \(A\) onto \(B\)).

Image of a set.

\[ F(A) \;=\; F{}''A \;=\; \operatorname{ran}(F \restriction A). \]

The notation \(F(A)\) is standard but ambiguous when \(A\) happens to be both a member and a subset of \(\operatorname{dom}(F)\) — e.g. with \(\operatorname{dom}(F) = 3 = \{0, 1, 2\}\), is \(F(2)\) the value at \(2\) or the image of \(\{0, 1\}\)? Write \(F{}''A\) when this could be confused.

Building relations and functions

The axioms used so far (Extensionality, Comprehension, Pairing, Union) do not let us build many relations. The obvious source is the Cartesian product

\[ A \times B \;=\; \{\,\langle x, y\rangle : x \in A \wedge y \in B\,\}, \]

after which any relation between \(A\) and \(B\) is a subset of \(A \times B\), cut out by Comprehension. So everything reduces to: does \(A \times B\) exist?

First method: Power Set

If \(x \in A\) and \(y \in B\) then \(\{x\}, \{x, y\} \subseteq A \cup B\), so \(\langle x, y\rangle = \bigl\{\{x\}, \{x, y\}\bigr\} \in \mathcal{P}\bigl(\mathcal{P}(A \cup B)\bigr)\). Hence

\[ A \times B \;=\; \bigl\{\, w \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(A \cup B)) : \exists x \in A\;\exists y \in B\;(w = \langle x, y\rangle)\,\bigr\} \]

exists by Power Set (twice) and Comprehension.

This works, but it is a little “wasteful” in terms of cardinality. The sets

\[ \{0\} \times \omega, \qquad \operatorname{id}_\omega = \{\,\langle x, x\rangle : x \in \omega\,\} \]

have the same “size” as \(\omega\), yet the justification above manufactures them as subsets of \(\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\omega))\) — a set of size \(2^{2^{\aleph_0}}\). We are invoking the existence of a huge set to produce a countable one.

Second method: Replacement

General method. Suppose \(\forall x \in A\;\exists! y\;\varphi(x, y)\). Then Replacement gives a set \(B\) with \(\forall x \in A\;\exists y \in B\;\varphi(x, y)\), and Comprehension trims it to \(\{\,y \in B : \exists x \in A\;\varphi(x, y)\,\} = \{\,y : \exists x \in A\;\varphi(x, y)\,\}\). So the image of a set under a definable single-valued relation is a set.

Apply this to build \(A \times B\) without Power Set. Fix \(b \in B\) and let

\[ \varphi(x, y) \;\equiv\; y = \langle x, b\rangle . \]

Then \(\forall x \in A\;\exists! y\;\varphi(x, y)\), so by Replacement + Comprehension the set

\[ A \times \{b\} \;=\; \{\,\langle x, b\rangle : x \in A\,\} \]

exists. Applying Replacement again — now over \(b \in B\) — gives

\[ S \;:=\; \bigl\{\, A \times \{b\} : b \in B\,\bigr\}, \]

and finally \(A \times B = \bigcup S\) by Union.

This argument never opened up \(\langle x, b\rangle\); it used only that \(\langle x, b\rangle\) is some set determined by \(x\) and \(b\). So it proves \(A \times B\) (and also \(\operatorname{dom}(R)\), \(\operatorname{ran}(R)\)) exist for any definition of the ordered pair.

Packaging the general method as a statement about functions:

Lemma. If \(\forall x \in A\;\exists! y\;\varphi(x, y)\), then there is a function \(f\) with \(\operatorname{dom}(f) = A\) such that for each \(x \in A\), \(f(x)\) is the unique \(y\) with \(\varphi(x, y)\).

Proof. Fix \(B\) from Replacement and set \(f = \{\,\langle x, y\rangle \in A \times B : \varphi(x, y)\,\}\). \(\square\)

Inverses, composition, isomorphism

\(R^{-1} := \{\,\langle y, x\rangle : \langle x, y\rangle \in R\,\}\)
a subset of \(\operatorname{ran}(R) \times \operatorname{dom}(R)\). If \(f\) is a function, \(f^{-1}\) is a function iff \(f\) is \(1\)-\(1\).
\(G \circ F := \{\,\langle x, z\rangle : \exists y\,(\langle x, y\rangle \in F \wedge \langle y, z\rangle \in G)\,\}\)
when \(F, G\) are functions with \(\operatorname{ran}(F) \subseteq \operatorname{dom}(G)\), this is just \((G \circ F)(x) = G(F(x))\).

Isomorphism. Let \(R\) be a binary relation on \(A\) and \(S\) a binary relation on \(B\). Then \(F\) is an isomorphism from \((A, R)\) onto \((B, S)\) iff

\[ F : A \xrightarrow[\text{onto}]{1\text{-}1} B \qquad\text{and}\qquad \forall x, y \in A\;\bigl[\,x \mathbin{R} y \leftrightarrow F(x) \mathbin{S} F(y)\,\bigr]. \]

We write \((A, R) \cong (B, S)\) when such an \(F\) exists. The definition makes sense for arbitrary relations; for now we use it for orderings.

Equivalence relations and quotients

Let \(R\) be an equivalence relation on a set \(A\), and \(x \in A\). Define

\[ [x] \;:=\; \{\,y \in A : x \mathbin{R} y\,\} \qquad\text{(Comprehension)}, \]

the equivalence class of \(x\), and

\[ A / R \;:=\; \bigl\{\, [x] : x \in A\,\bigr\}. \]

To see \(A/R\) is a set, apply the general method with \(\varphi(x, y) \equiv y = [x]\): by Lemma I.7.9 there is a function \(f\) with \(\operatorname{dom}(f) = A\) and \(f(x) = [x]\) — the quotient map \(x \mapsto [x]\) — and then \(A/R = \operatorname{ran}(f)\).

This is the standard justification, in books on group theory and topology, for forming a quotient of a structure by an equivalence relation; the extra structure on \(A/R\) is defined separately.

Object theory versus metatheory

The informal notions “relation” and “function” get two representations in set theory: as sets (objects of the formal theory ZFC), and as abbreviations in the metatheory.

As sets

A relation is a specific kind of set, handled inside ZFC. The defining property is a formula of the object theory — for example

\[ \operatorname{Rel}(R) \;:\equiv\; \forall u \in R\;\exists y, z\;\bigl(u = \langle y, z\rangle\bigr). \]

We then prove facts about such sets as theorems of ZFC, e.g.

\[ \mathrm{ZFC} \;\vdash\; \operatorname{Rel}(R) \rightarrow \exists z\,\bigl(z = \operatorname{dom}(R)\bigr), \]

using the justification given earlier. A concrete function such as \(f = \{\langle 1, 2\rangle, \langle 2, 2\rangle, \langle 3, 1\rangle\}\) is likewise a set formally defined in ZFC.

As abbreviations in the metatheory

By contrast, \(\in\), \(=\), \(\subseteq\) are informal relations — a different kind of animal. The “relation”

\[ S \;=\; \{\,p : \exists x, y\;(p = \langle x, y\rangle \wedge x \subseteq y)\,\} \]

does not exist: it is a proper class, since \(\operatorname{dom}(S)\) would be the universe \(V\). We view \(x \subseteq y\) instead as an abbreviation in the metatheory for \(\forall z\,(z \in x \rightarrow z \in y)\); likewise \((A, R) \cong (B, S)\) abbreviates a longer statement. Membership and equality, \(\in\) and \(=\), are already primitive symbols.

Many properties from the terminology list still make sense for such pseudorelations, by plugging them into the defining formula. For instance \(\in\) totally orders \(3 = \{0, 1, 2\}\) (i.e. \(\forall x, y, z \in 3\,[x \in y \wedge y \in z \rightarrow x \in z]\), plus trichotomy), while \(\subseteq\) is transitive on all of \(V\) but not a total order of \(3\), and \(\in\) is not transitive on \(V\) (since \(0 \in \{0\} \in \{\{0\}\}\) but \(0 \notin \{\{0\}\}\)).

Similarly for functions: \(\bigcup : V \to V\) and \(\mathcal{P} : V \to V\) are pseudofunctions. There is no set of pairs \(\bigcup = \{\langle \mathcal{F}, \bigcup \mathcal{F}\rangle : \mathcal{F} \in V\}\); instead there is a formula \(\varphi(\mathcal{F}, Z)\) saying “\(Z\) is the union of the members of \(\mathcal{F}\)”, we prove \(\forall \mathcal{F}\;\exists! Z\;\varphi(\mathcal{F}, Z)\), and \(\bigcup \mathcal{F}\) denotes that \(Z\). Function properties still apply as abbreviations — e.g. “\(\bigcup\) is not \(1\)-\(1\)” is the formula

\[ \exists x_1, x_2, y\;\bigl[\varphi(x_1, y) \wedge \varphi(x_2, y) \wedge x_1 \neq x_2\bigr]. \]
