A Gallery of Toposes

Which properties of \(\mathcal{S}\) survive? Slices, \(G\)-sets, presheaves, sheaves, and toposes from logic

Author
Affiliation

Jan Reimann

Penn State — Math 558

Last time we arrived at the definition of an elementary topos: a category with finite limits, exponentials, and a subobject classifier \(\true : 1 \to \Omega\). The category \(\mathcal{S}\) of abstract sets is one example. Today we look at many others. The guiding question is:

Which of the extra properties of \(\mathcal{S}\) survive in a given topos?

The answer shows that the topos axioms capture a very general notion of “set” — sets that vary, move, carry symmetry, or are only locally defined — and that the classical features of \(\mathcal{S}\) are genuinely additional axioms.

What is special about \(\mathcal{S}\) [≈ 5 min]

Recall the properties of \(\mathcal{S}\) that go beyond the topos axioms. For a topos \(\mathcal{E}\):

Well-pointed
\(0 \not\cong 1\), and \(1\) separates maps: if \(f \neq g : A \to B\), there is \(a : 1 \to A\) with \(fa \neq ga\).
Boolean
Every part has a complement; equivalently \([\true, \mathrm{false}] : 1 + 1 \to \Omega\) is an isomorphism.
Two-valued
\(\Omega\) has exactly two elements \(1 \to \Omega\) (equivalently, \(1\) has exactly two parts, \(0\) and \(1\)).
Natural numbers object
An object \(\mathbb{N}\) with \(0 : 1 \to \mathbb{N}\), \(s : \mathbb{N}\to \mathbb{N}\) such that for every \(x_0 : 1 \to X\), \(f : X \to X\) there is a unique \(h : \mathbb{N}\to X\) with \(h0 = x_0\) and \(hs = fh\).
Choice
Every epimorphism has a section.

“Two-valued” was not on our list last time. It is weaker than Boolean, and the examples below show that the two notions really differ.

In any topos, the parts of an object \(X\) form a lattice \(\Sub(X)\) (intersections are pullbacks, unions are images of sums). Each part \(A\) has a pseudo-complement \(\neg A\), the largest part disjoint from \(A\), and \(\Sub(X)\) is a Heyting algebra. Booleanness says \(A \vee \neg A = X\) for all parts \(A\), i.e. the internal logic satisfies excluded middle.

Two classical theorems connect the properties. We state them without proof (see Goldblatt, Topoi, or Mac Lane–Moerdijk).

Theorem 1 (Two roads to classical logic)
Let \(\mathcal{E}\) be a topos.

  1. If \(\mathcal{E}\) is well-pointed, then \(\mathcal{E}\) is Boolean (and two-valued).
  2. (Diaconescu) If every epimorphism in \(\mathcal{E}\) splits, then \(\mathcal{E}\) is Boolean.

Booleanness is the hinge. A topos that fails excluded middle fails both well-pointedness and choice. In \(\mathcal{S}\), the classicality of logic is not a separate assumption; it is forced by choice alone, and also by well-pointedness alone.

Part 2 is the categorical form of a fact you may know from set theory: over a constructive base theory, \(\mathsf{AC}\) implies the law of excluded middle.

The easy ones [≈ 7 min]

Finite sets

\(\FinSet\), the full subcategory of finite sets, is a topos: finite limits of finite sets are finite, \(Y^B\) is finite, and \(\Omega = 2\). It is well-pointed, Boolean, and satisfies choice (without any appeal to \(\mathsf{AC}\): a surjection between finite sets has a section by induction).

But it has no natural numbers object (Exercise 1). The axiom of infinity is not part of being a topos.

The trivial topos

The category \(\mathbf{1}\) with one object and one arrow is a topos: its single object is terminal, initial, \(\Omega\), and every exponential. Here \(0 \cong 1\). Every statement is “true” — which is why nondegeneracy \(0 \not\cong 1\) appears in the definition of well-pointedness.

Slices: sets over \(X\)

We met \(\mathcal{S}/X\) last time: an object is a map \(\alpha : A \to X\), i.e. an \(X\)-indexed family of sets \(A_x = \alpha^{-1}(x)\), and everything is computed fiberwise: \[ (A \times_X B)_x = A_x \times B_x, \qquad (Y^B)_x = Y_x^{B_x}, \qquad \Omega = (X \times 2 \to X), \quad \Omega_x = 2 . \]

  • Terminal object: \(1_X : X \to X\), one point in every fiber.
  • Elements \(1 \to A\) are sections of \(\alpha\): a choice of one point in every fiber.
  • Not well-pointed (for \(|X| \ge 2\)): if one fiber of \(A\) is empty, \(A\) has no elements at all, yet if another fiber has two points, \(A\) has a non-identity automorphism (swap them).
  • Not two-valued: truth values \(1 \to \Omega\) are maps \(X \to 2\); there are \(2^{|X|}\) of them.
  • Boolean, with choice, and with a natural numbers object \(X \times \mathbb{N}\to X\): all fiberwise.

So: Boolean, but not well-pointed. “True” is now true at some indices and false at others.

Theorem 2 (Fundamental theorem of topos theory)
If \(\mathcal{E}\) is a topos and \(X\) an object of \(\mathcal{E}\), then \(\mathcal{E}/X\) is a topos.

TipLooking ahead: dependent types

An object of \(\mathcal{E}/X\) is a “type depending on \(x : X\).” Pulling back along \(f : X' \to X\) is substitution, and it has a left adjoint \(\Sigma_f\) and a right adjoint \(\Pi_f\), which are exactly the dependent sum and dependent product of Martin-Löf type theory. This is the interpretation of type theory in locally cartesian closed categories that we return to in Week 14.

\(G\)-sets: Boolean without choice [≈ 6 min]

Let \(G\) be a group. An object of \(\mathcal{S}^G\) is a set with a \(G\)-action, a map is an equivariant map. Limits are computed on underlying sets with the induced action; the exponential \(Y^X\) is the set of all maps with \((g\cdot f)(x) = g\, f(g^{-1}x)\) (last time).

  • Subobject classifier. A part is a \(G\)-invariant subset. Since \(G\) is a group, the complement of an invariant subset is invariant. So \(\Omega = 2\) with the trivial action, and \(\mathcal{S}^G\) is Boolean and two-valued.
  • Not well-pointed. Elements \(1 \to X\) are fixed points. If \(G \neq 1\), the action of \(G\) on itself by translation has no fixed points but many automorphisms.
  • Choice fails. The unique map \(G \to 1\) is surjective, hence an epimorphism. A section \(1 \to G\) would be a fixed point. There is none.

So Diaconescu’s theorem has no converse: Boolean does not imply choice.

TipConnection to Week 5

The first independence proofs for \(\mathsf{AC}\) (Fraenkel, Mostowski) used permutation models: a universe of sets built over atoms, keeping only the sets that are fixed by “enough” permutations of the atoms. These models can be presented as toposes of \(G\)-sets for a group \(G\) of permutations of the atoms (with a topology on \(G\), and only actions with open stabilizers). What we just saw — no fixed point, hence no section — is the simplest instance of why choice fails there.

Presheaf toposes [≈ 17 min]

\(\mathcal{S}/X\) and \(\mathcal{S}^G\) are both special cases of one construction.

NoteDefinition: presheaves

Let \(C\) be a small category. A presheaf on \(C\) is a functor \(P : C^{op} \to \mathcal{S}\); for \(x \in P(c)\) and \(f : d \to c\) we write \(x \cdot f := P(f)(x) \in P(d)\) (“restriction of \(x\) along \(f\)”). Maps of presheaves are natural transformations. The category of presheaves is written \(\Psh{C}\).

For each object \(c\), the representable presheaf is \(\yon c := C(-, c)\).

Limits of presheaves are computed pointwise: \((P \times Q)(c) = P(c) \times Q(c)\), and so on. A map of presheaves is mono iff each component is injective, and epi iff each component is surjective. So a part of \(P\) is a family of subsets \(A(c) \subseteq P(c)\) closed under restriction.

Guessing \(\Omega\): let the axioms tell you

How could we find \(\Omega\)? Suppose it exists. We need one fact about representables.

Lemma 1 (Maps out of a representable)
For every presheaf \(P\) and object \(c\), the map \(\NatT(\yon c, P) \to P(c)\), \(\alpha \mapsto \alpha_c(1_c)\), is a bijection.

NoteProof

Proof. Given \(\alpha\) and \(f : d \to c\), naturality of \(\alpha\) applied to \(f\) and the element \(1_c \in \yon c(c)\) gives \[ \alpha_d(f) = \alpha_d(1_c \circ f) = \alpha_c(1_c)\cdot f . \] So \(\alpha\) is determined by \(x := \alpha_c(1_c)\). Conversely, for any \(x \in P(c)\), the formula \(\alpha_d(f) := x \cdot f\) defines a natural transformation (check naturality using that \(P\) is a functor). This is the bootstrap trick from last time — everything is determined by where the identity goes — and it is a special case of the Yoneda lemma, which we will meet properly soon.

Now combine the lemma with the subobject classifier axiom: \[ \Omega(c) \;\cong\; \NatT(\yon c, \Omega) \;\cong\; \Sub(\yon c). \] So \(\Omega(c)\) must be the set of parts of \(\yon c\). A part of \(\yon c\) is a set \(S\) of arrows with codomain \(c\) that is closed under precomposition: if \(f \in S\) then \(f \circ g \in S\). Such a set is called a sieve on \(c\). Exactly the same reasoning determines the exponential: \((Y^X)(c) \cong \NatT(\yon c, Y^X) \cong \NatT(\yon c \times X, Y)\).

Theorem 3 (Presheaf toposes)
For every small category \(C\), \(\Psh{C}\) is a topos, with

  • \(\Omega(c) = \{ S : S \text{ a sieve on } c\}\), and for \(f : d \to c\), \(S \cdot f = \{ g : f \circ g \in S\}\);
  • $_c = $ the maximal sieve (all arrows into \(c\));
  • for a part \(A \subseteq X\), the characteristic map \[ \chi_c(x) = \{\, f : d \to c \;:\; x \cdot f \in A(d) \,\}; \]
  • \((Y^X)(c) = \NatT(\yon c \times X, Y)\);
  • natural numbers object: the constant presheaf with value \(\mathbb{N}\).

The characteristic map has a nice reading: \(\chi(x)\) records all the ways in which \(x\) eventually ends up in \(A\). In \(\mathcal{S}\) there is only one “way” (\(1_c\)), so \(\chi(x)\) is just yes or no.

NoteFact: when is a presheaf topos Boolean?

\(\Psh{C}\) is Boolean if and only if \(C\) is a groupoid (every arrow invertible).

The recipe in action

index category \(C\) presheaves \(\Psh{C}\) \(\Omega\)
one object, only the identity \(\mathcal{S}\) \(2\)
a set \(X\), only identities families \((A_x)_{x\in X}\), i.e. \(\mathcal{S}/X\) \(2\) in each fiber
a group \(G\), one object \(G\)-sets \(2\), trivial action
a monoid \(M\), one object (right) \(M\)-sets right ideals of \(M\)
the monoid \((\mathbb{N},+)\) sets with an endomap \(\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}\), \(n \mapsto n-1\), \(0 \mapsto 0\)
\(V \rightrightarrows E\) (arrows \(s, t\)) directed graphs 2 vertices, 5 edges
the simplex category \(\Delta\) simplicial sets (the Week 14 model of HoTT)

A few checks:

  • For a monoid \(M\) (one object, arrows \(= M\), composition \(=\) multiplication), a sieve is a subset \(S \subseteq M\) with \(S \cdot M \subseteq S\): a right ideal. (For left actions, as last time, one gets left ideals.) For a group, the only right ideals are \(\varnothing\) and \(G\), recovering \(\Omega = 2\).
  • For \((\mathbb{N}, +)\) the ideals are \(\varnothing\) and \(k + \mathbb{N}\) for \(k \in \mathbb{N}\). Write \(\infty\) for \(\varnothing\) and \(k\) for \(k + \mathbb{N}\). Acting by \(1\) sends \(k + \mathbb{N}\) to \(\{n : 1 + n \in k + \mathbb{N}\} = (k-1)+\mathbb{N}\) (or \(\mathbb{N}\) if \(k = 0\)). This is the \(\Omega\) for sets with an endomap: \(\chi(x)\) is the time it takes \(x\) to enter \(A\).
  • For graphs, a presheaf \(X\) has a set of vertices \(X(V)\), a set of edges \(X(E)\), and source and target maps \(X(E) \to X(V)\). Arrows into \(V\): only \(1_V\), so there are two sieves on \(V\). Arrows into \(E\): \(1_E, s, t\), giving five sieves: \(\{1_E, s, t\}, \{s,t\}, \{s\}, \{t\}, \varnothing\). Read: the edge is in the subgraph; it is not, but both endpoints are; only the source is; only the target is; neither endpoint is.

The Sierpiński topos, by hand

The smallest non-Boolean example deserves a complete computation. Let \(C\) be the category with two objects \(0, 1\) and a single non-identity arrow \(u : 1 \to 0\). A presheaf on \(C\) is just a map \[ X_0 \xrightarrow{\ \xi\ } X_1 , \] and a morphism is a commuting square. Think of \(X_0\) as “what exists now,” \(X_1\) as “what exists later,” and \(\xi\) as “how things develop.” A part of \(X\) is a pair \(A_0 \subseteq X_0\), \(A_1 \subseteq X_1\) with \(\xi(A_0) \subseteq A_1\): once in \(A\), always in \(A\).

Truth values. Arrows into \(1\): only \(1_1\), so \(\Omega_1 = \{\top, \bot\}\). Arrows into \(0\): \(1_0\) and \(u\), and the sieves are \(\{1_0, u\}\), \(\{u\}\), \(\varnothing\). So \[ \Omega_0 = \{\top, \tfrac12, \bot\} \xrightarrow{\quad} \Omega_1 = \{\top, \bot\}, \qquad \top \mapsto \top,\ \ \tfrac12 \mapsto \top,\ \ \bot \mapsto \bot . \] For a part \(A \subseteq X\) and \(a \in X_0\), \[ \chi_0(a) = \begin{cases} \top & a \in A_0 \quad (\text{true now}),\\ \tfrac12 & a \notin A_0 \text{ but } \xi(a) \in A_1 \quad (\text{not yet, but later}),\\ \bot & \xi(a) \notin A_1 \quad (\text{never}). \end{cases} \] You can verify directly that this classifies parts (Exercise 2).

Excluded middle fails. Take \(X = 1 = (1 \to 1)\) and the part \(A = (\varnothing, 1)\): the proposition “it is not true now, but it will be.” The pseudo-complement \(\neg A\) must be disjoint from \(A\), so \((\neg A)_1 = \varnothing\), hence \((\neg A)_0 = \varnothing\) as well. Therefore \[ \neg A = 0, \qquad A \vee \neg A = A \neq 1, \qquad \neg\neg A = 1 \neq A . \] \(A\) is not false (it is not refuted), and yet not true. Its characteristic map is the truth value \((\tfrac12, \top) : 1 \to \Omega\).

The rest of the profile:

  • Three global truth values \((\top,\top), (\tfrac12, \top), (\bot, \bot)\): not two-valued.
  • Not well-pointed. An element \(1 \to X\) is just an \(a \in X_0\). The object \(\varnothing \to 2\) has no elements, but two different automorphisms.
  • Choice fails, by Diaconescu — or directly (Exercise 2).
TipKripke models

This is exactly the semantics of intuitionistic logic by Kripke models with two worlds. Functors \(P \to \mathcal{S}\) on a poset \(P\) (i.e. presheaves on \(P^{op}\)) are Kripke models over \(P\): a statement is “true at stage \(p\)” if it holds at \(p\) and at all later stages, and \(\neg\varphi\) is true at \(p\) if \(\varphi\) fails at every later stage. Presheaves on \((\mathbb{N},\le)^{op}\) are “sets through discrete time.”

Sheaves on a space [≈ 10 min]

Let \(X\) be a topological space and \(\mathcal{O}(X)\) its poset of open sets, viewed as a category (\(V \to U\) iff \(V \subseteq U\)). A presheaf on \(\mathcal{O}(X)\) assigns to each open \(U\) a set \(F(U)\) (“sections over \(U\)”) with restriction maps \(s \mapsto s|_V\) for \(V \subseteq U\). Example: \(F(U) = C(U, \mathbb{R})\).

NoteDefinition: sheaf

A presheaf \(F\) on \(\mathcal{O}(X)\) is a sheaf if whenever \(U = \bigcup_i U_i\) and \(s_i \in F(U_i)\) agree on overlaps (\(s_i|_{U_i\cap U_j} = s_j|_{U_i \cap U_j}\)), there is a unique \(s \in F(U)\) with \(s|_{U_i} = s_i\) for all \(i\).

Continuous functions form a sheaf (“continuity is local”); bounded functions do not. The category \(\Sh(X)\) of sheaves is a topos, with \[ \Omega(U) = \{ V : V \subseteq U \text{ open}\}, \qquad V|_W = V \cap W, \qquad \true_U = U, \] and for a subsheaf \(A \subseteq F\) and \(s \in F(U)\), \[ \chi_U(s) = \bigcup\{ W \subseteq U \text{ open} : s|_W \in A(W) \} : \] the truth value of “\(s \in A\)” is the largest open set on which it holds.

Global truth values are the open sets of \(X\), and the logic of truth values is the Heyting algebra \(\mathcal{O}(X)\): \[ U \wedge V = U \cap V, \qquad U \vee V = U \cup V, \qquad \neg U = \operatorname{int}(X \setminus U). \] In \(\mathbb{R}\), with \(U = (0, \infty)\): \(\neg U = (-\infty, 0)\), so \(U \vee \neg U = \mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\} \neq \mathbb{R}\).

The real numbers in \(\Sh(\mathbb{R})\)

\(\Sh(X)\) has a real numbers object (constructed by Dedekind cuts internally), and it turns out to be the sheaf of continuous real-valued functions, \(U \mapsto C(U, \mathbb{R})\). So an “internal real number” over \(\mathbb{R}\) is a continuous function, e.g. the identity \(x : \mathbb{R}\to \mathbb{R}\). Compute truth values: \[ [\![x < 0]\!] = (-\infty, 0), \qquad [\![x = 0]\!] = \operatorname{int}\{0\} = \varnothing, \qquad [\![x > 0]\!] = (0, \infty). \] Hence \[ [\![\,x < 0 \;\vee\; x = 0 \;\vee\; x > 0\,]\!] = \mathbb{R}\setminus \{0\} \neq \mathbb{R}. \] Trichotomy fails for the internal reals. This is not a pathology but a feature: it is the internal reflection of the fact that a continuous function cannot be split, continuously, into “negative,” “zero,” and “positive” cases. Constructive analysis is the mathematics that is valid in all such toposes.

Grothendieck toposes. Replacing \(\mathcal{O}(X)\) by a category with a suitable notion of “covering” (a site) gives the Grothendieck toposes, the setting of modern algebraic geometry. \(\mathcal{S}\), \(\mathcal{S}/X\), \(G\)-sets, all presheaf toposes and all \(\Sh(X)\) are Grothendieck toposes. In fact the Sierpiński topos is \(\Sh\) of the Sierpiński space (Exercise 3), and \(\mathcal{S}/X\) is \(\Sh\) of the discrete space \(X\).

Toposes from logic [≈ 8 min]

The last two examples are only surveyed: their constructions take more than a lecture each.

Forcing as sheaves

Cohen’s proof that \(\mathsf{CH}\) is independent of \(\mathsf{ZFC}\) (Week 5) can be redone in topos theory (Tierney; Mac Lane–Moerdijk, Ch. VI). Take the poset \(P\) of Cohen’s forcing conditions and form the topos \(\Sh_{\neg\neg}(P)\) of sheaves for the “dense” covering relation. This Cohen topos is Boolean, has a natural numbers object, and internally there is an object strictly between \(\mathbb{N}\) and \(\Omega^{\mathbb{N}}\) in size: \(\mathsf{CH}\) fails. Similarly, Boolean-valued models \(V^{\mathbb{B}}\) correspond to sheaves on a complete Boolean algebra \(\mathbb{B}\). Forcing is “sets varying over conditions.”

Realizability: the effective topos

Hyland’s effective topos \(\Eff\) is built from Kleene’s realizability: a statement is true when some program (an index of a computable function) “realizes” it. In \(\Eff\):

  • the natural numbers object is the ordinary \(\mathbb{N}\), but every function \(\mathbb{N}\to \mathbb{N}\) is computable (internally, “Church’s thesis” holds);
  • it is two-valued but not Boolean: excluded middle would require deciding the halting problem;
  • it is not a Grothendieck topos: it does not arise from sheaves on any site.

Realizability is the ancestor of the “proofs as programs” reading of logic we meet in Week 10: a proof of \(\forall n\, \exists m\, \varphi(n,m)\) in \(\Eff\) comes with an algorithm computing \(m\) from \(n\).

Non-examples [≈ 4 min]

Topos theory gives quick tests for showing a category is not a topos. In a topos, every map that is both mono and epi is an isomorphism, the initial object is strict, and \(- \times B\) preserves colimits (it is a left adjoint).

  • \(\mathbf{Pos}\): cartesian closed, but the identity map from the 2-element discrete poset to the 2-element chain is mono and epi without being an iso.
  • \(\mathbf{Top}\): not even cartesian closed. Not every \(- \times B\) preserves quotients (e.g. \(B = \mathbb{Q}\)).
  • \(\mathbf{Grp}\): no subobject classifier. If \(\true : 1 \to \Omega\) existed, every subgroup would be the pullback of \(\true\) along a homomorphism, i.e. a kernel, hence normal. But \(S_3\) has non-normal subgroups.
  • \(\mathcal{S}^{op}\): has finite limits and colimits, but its initial object (the set \(1\)) is not strict.

Reading the table.

Homework

Exercise 1 Show that \(\FinSet\) has no natural numbers object. (Hint: if \((N, 0, s)\) were one with \(|N| = n\), use the cyclic set \(\mathbb{Z}/(n+1)\) with \(f(k) = k+1\) and the pigeonhole principle on \(0, s0, \dots, s^n 0\).)

Exercise 2 The Sierpiński topos.

  1. Verify directly that \(\Omega = (\Omega_0 \to \Omega_1)\) with \(\true = (\top, \top)\) classifies parts: the formula for \(\chi_0\) gives a morphism of presheaves, the pullback of \(\true\) along it is \(A\), and it is the only such morphism.

  2. Let \(E = (2 \xrightarrow{\,1\,} 2)\) and \(B = (2 \to 1)\). Show that \((1_2, !) : E \to B\) is an epimorphism with no section.

  3. Compute \(\neg\neg A\) for every part \(A\) of the object \((2 \to 1)\), where both points of \(X_0\) map to the single point of \(X_1\). Which parts satisfy \(\neg\neg A = A\)?

Exercise 3 The Sierpiński space has points \(\{a, b\}\) and open sets \(\varnothing, \{b\}, \{a, b\}\). Show that a sheaf on it amounts to a map \(F(\{a,b\}) \to F(\{b\})\), so that its sheaf topos is the Sierpiński topos. Compare with the two-point discrete space: which properties in the summary table change?

Exercise 4 Directed graphs. Using Theorem 3, draw \(\Omega\) for graphs: its two vertices, its five edges, and their sources and targets. How many elements \(1 \to \Omega\) are there (the terminal graph is one vertex with one loop)? Find a subgraph without a complement.

Exercise 5 In \(\Sh(\mathbb{R})\), compute \(\neg\neg U\) for an open set \(U \subseteq \mathbb{R}\). Find \(U\) with \(\neg\neg U \neq U\).

Exercise 6 (Optional.) Let \(M\) be a monoid. Show that the topos of right \(M\)-sets is Boolean iff \(M\) is a group. (Hint: if \(m\) has no right inverse, consider the part \(mM\) of the representable \(M\), and show that \(1\) lies neither in \(mM\) nor in \(\neg(mM)\).)
