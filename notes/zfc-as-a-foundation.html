ZFC as a Foundational Theory

Metatheory, the finitistic standpoint, and the axioms

Author
Affiliation

Jan Reimann

Penn State — Math 558

These notes accompany Lecture 3. They expand the handwritten notes and follow Kunen, The Foundations of Mathematics, §0.4 and §I.1–I.6. Where Lecture 2 ended — Hilbert’s program — is where we begin.

From Hilbert’s program to a working foundation

Hilbert’s response to the foundational crisis was formalism: not the claim that mathematics is a meaningless game with symbols, but a method. Take a mathematical theory, write its axioms in a formal language, and study its formal proof-structure with modest, uncontroversial means. This method forces a distinction that will organize everything that follows.

Metatheory and object theory

Formalism introduces a split between two levels of discourse.

The two levels
Object theory (formal theory) Metatheory
Example a theory of arithmetic, or ZFC itself our reasoning about that theory
Status a combinatorial object: sets of symbols, strings, rules ordinary mathematical reasoning
Language a fixed formal language, e.g. first-order logic informal natural language
“Proof” a finite symbolic derivation an intuitively convincing argument

The object theory is formalized

To say the object theory is formalized means two things:

  • its axioms are expressed in a formal language (for us, first-order logic with ∈);
  • its theorems are deduced in a purely symbolic fashion — as Kleene put it, like a list of chess moves written in chess notation. Whether a string is a correct proof is decided by inspecting symbols, not by understanding meaning.

A consequence Hilbert exploited: once proofs are finite strings manipulated by explicit rules, the combinatorics of proof becomes essentially elementary arithmetic. Reasoning about proofs is reasoning about finite syntactic objects.

Reasoning in the metatheory

The metatheory is where we actually work when we prove things about the formal system. That reasoning is:

  • informal — carried out in natural language;
  • intuitively clear and convincing — it must persuade without itself being formalized;
  • finitistic — close to elementary number theory. We do not treat infinite totalities as completed wholes unless they can be clearly and explicitly specified. In flavour this is the intuitionist / constructivist standpoint: an existence claim should come with a construction.

A finitistic statement, and a non-statement

Consider the claim

\[ P \text{ and } \mathbb{N}\setminus P \text{ are both infinite,} \qquad P = \{\text{primes}\}. \]

This is a statement involving completed infinite sets (i.e. it is a statement about the natural numbers as a whole). But it can be rewritten finitistically:

For every natural number \(n > 1\) there is a composite number \(c\) with \(n < c \le 2n\), and a prime \(p\) with \(n < p \le n! + 1\).

Both witnesses are bounded (and even can be explicitely defined). This is the kind of fact the metatheory is entitled to assert.

Contrast this with

\[ |\mathbb{R}| = |\mathbb{C}|, \]

which is meaningless in the metatheory — it is a statement about the cardinality of uncountable sets, with no finitistic content. It only makes sense inside an object theory such as ZFC.

The foundational loop: formalizing the metatheory

For a foundational theory such as ZFC, the formal (object) theory should be strong enough to formalize its own metatheory. Otherwise the reasoning we use to study ZFC would sit outside the foundation ZFC claims to provide.

This motivates a thesis about ZFC — a property we would want a foundation to have:

Thesis. Every finitistic fact can be formalized and proved within ZFC.

Making this precise takes care, because most everyday mathematical statements are not literally in the language of ZFC. For instance

\[ \forall x \in \mathbb{R}\;\bigl(x^{3}\cdot x^{5} = x^{8}\bigr) \]

uses \(\mathbb{R}\), multiplication, and exponentiation — none of which are primitive symbols of a language whose only non-logical symbol is ∈. Such statements enter ZFC through codings / representations.

Example: an arithmetic fact and its formal shadow

“\(2 + 8 = 10\)” is a fact of the metatheory. Inside ZFC we find:

  • a representation of each natural number as a specific set — write \(\ulcorner 2 \urcorner\), \(\ulcorner 8 \urcorner\), \(\ulcorner 10 \urcorner\);
  • a defined binary function symbol \(+\) for addition of such representations;

and then we can prove

\[ \mathrm{ZFC} \;\vdash\; \ulcorner 2 \urcorner + \ulcorner 8 \urcorner = \ulcorner 10 \urcorner . \]

The metatheoretic fact and its formal counterpart are different objects; the thesis says the second always tracks the first.

ZFC as a foundational theory

The claim that ZFC founds mathematics rests on two theses:

  1. All abstract mathematical concepts are set-theoretical.
  2. All concrete mathematical objects are specific sets.

If one does not accept formalism, the “are” can be weakened to “have set-theoretical representations.”

Everything reduces to sets.

Example: what a function is

\[ \text{function} \;\cong\; \text{graph} \;\cong\; \text{a set of ordered pairs } f \text{ with } \forall x, y, z\,\bigl((x,y)\in f \wedge (x,z)\in f \rightarrow y = z\bigr). \]

This pushes the question back to ordered pairs, which are themselves described as special kinds of sets — the standard choice being

\[ (x, y) := \bigl\{\{x\}, \{x, y\}\bigr\}, \]

for which one verifies \((x,y) = (x',y') \rightarrow (x = x' \wedge y = y')\).

The special nature of the ZFC axioms

Kunen distinguishes axioms used as statements of faith (obviously true facts, as the Greeks viewed Euclid’s postulates) from definitional axioms (merely delimiting a useful class of structures, as with the group axioms).

You can read the ZFC axioms in the first sense: To a Platonist, these might appear as obviously true facts. But we have seen that an all too careless treatment of the notion of set can lead to contradictions. However, one can, independent of the ontological status of the axioms, read ZFC as the theory of everything: one postulates the ZFC axioms once and for all, and every other axiom system — groups, rings, topological spaces — is defined inside it.

What we will do

  • The basic level. State the axioms and interpret them; work out what exactly the representations of \(3\) and of \(\mathbb{R}\) are. (Following von Neumann, \(0 = \varnothing\), \(1 = \{\varnothing\}\), \(2 = \{0,1\}\), \(3 = \{0,1,2\}\), …, \(\mathbb{N} = \omega\); and \(\mathbb{R}\) via Dedekind cuts.)
  • The advanced level. Draw out the consequences of the axioms for uncountable sets.

The axioms of ZFC

The language is first-order logic with a single binary relation symbol ∈ (and equality). There is one sort of variable: everything ranges over sets. In the Union and Replacement axioms below, the capital letters \(A, Y, \mathcal{F}, B\) are still just set variables — the capitals only aid reading.

Convention. Every axiom is understood to be universally quantified over its free variables (\(\forall x\,\forall y\,\dots\)).

Axiom 0 — Set Existence
\(\exists x\,(x = x)\)
Axiom 1 — Extensionality
\(\forall z\,(z \in x \leftrightarrow z \in y) \;\rightarrow\; x = y\)
A set is determined by its members.
Axiom 2 — Foundation
\(\exists y\,(y \in x) \;\rightarrow\; \exists y\,\bigl(y \in x \wedge \neg\exists z\,(z \in x \wedge z \in y)\bigr)\)
Every nonempty set has an ∈-minimal element; no infinite descending ∈-chains.
Axiom 3 — Comprehension (Separation) — scheme
For each formula \(\varphi\) with \(y\) not free: \(\exists y\,\forall x\,\bigl(x \in y \leftrightarrow x \in v \wedge \varphi(x)\bigr)\)
Subsets are carved out of a set \(v\) that already exists — the restriction to \(x \in v\) is what defuses Russell’s paradox (there is no universal set). There is one axiom per formula, so this is infinitely many axioms; that is harmless in the metatheory, since “being a formula” is a finitistically decidable property.
Axiom 4 — Pairing
\(\exists z\,(x \in z \wedge y \in z)\)
Axiom 5 — Union
\(\exists A\,\forall Y\,\forall x\, \bigl(x \in Y \wedge Y \in \mathcal{F} \;\rightarrow\; x \in A\bigr)\)
Axiom 6 — Replacement — scheme
For each formula \(\varphi\) with \(B\) not free: \(\forall x \in A\;\exists! y\,\varphi(x, y) \;\rightarrow\; \exists B\,\forall x \in A\;\exists y \in B\;\varphi(x, y)\)
The image of a set under a definable class function is a set. Here \(\exists! y\,\varphi(x,y)\) abbreviates \(\exists y\,\bigl(\varphi(x,y) \wedge \forall z\,(\varphi(x,z) \rightarrow y = z)\bigr)\). Again a scheme, and again finitistically unproblematic.

Some defined symbols

On the basis of Axioms 1, 3, 4, 5 we abbreviate:

\[ \begin{aligned} x \subseteq y \;&:\Longleftrightarrow\; \forall z\,(z \in x \rightarrow z \in y)\\[2pt] x = \varnothing \;&:\Longleftrightarrow\; \forall z\,(z \notin x)\\[2pt] y = S(x) \;&:\Longleftrightarrow\; \forall z\,(z \in y \leftrightarrow z \in x \vee z = x) \qquad\bigl(\,y = x \cup \{x\}\,\bigr)\\[2pt] y = v \cap w \;&:\Longleftrightarrow\; \forall x\,(x \in y \leftrightarrow x \in v \wedge x \in w)\\[2pt] \mathrm{SING}(x) \;&:\Longleftrightarrow\; \exists y \in x\;\forall z \in x\,(z = y) \end{aligned} \]

The remaining axioms are easier to state with these in hand.

Axiom 7 — Infinity
\(\exists x\,\bigl(\varnothing \in x \wedge \forall y \in x\,(S(y) \in x)\bigr)\)
There is a set containing \(\varnothing\) and closed under \(y \mapsto y \cup \{y\}\). Here “\(\varnothing \in x\)” abbreviates \(\exists y\,(y = \varnothing \wedge y \in x)\), and “\(S(y) \in x\)” abbreviates \(\exists z\,(z = S(y) \wedge z \in x)\).
Axiom 8 — Power Set
\(\exists y\,\forall z\,(z \subseteq x \rightarrow z \in y)\)
Axiom 9 — Choice (AC)
\(\varnothing \notin F \wedge \forall x \in F\;\forall y \in F\, (x \neq y \rightarrow x \cap y = \varnothing) \;\rightarrow\; \exists C\;\forall x \in F\;\mathrm{SING}(C \cap x)\)
For a family \(F\) of pairwise disjoint nonempty sets there is a set \(C\) meeting each member in exactly one point.

Named subsystems

System Axioms
ZFC 1–9
ZF 1–8 (no Choice)
ZC ZFC without Replacement (6)
Z ZF without Replacement (6)

Other important subsystems:

  • \(X^{-}\) — drop Foundation (2);
  • \(X{-}\mathrm{P}\) — drop Power Set (8);
  • \(X{-}\mathrm{Inf}\) — drop Infinity (7).

Most of elementary mathematics can be carried out in \(\mathrm{ZC}^{-}\). A minimal working fragment is Basic Set Theory:

\[ \mathrm{BST} \;=\; \text{Axioms } 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 \;+\; (6 \vee 8). \]
